Unit 0.1 — Probability, distributions & the quantile machinery behind the confidence levels

Before any risk model, one object: the loss random variable and the quantile you call a confidence level. Master it and the Leitfaden's 95 % / 99,9 %, its CVA scalars, and its op-risk factor stop being magic numbers.

Course 0 · Foundations Estimated: 10–14 h Lectures: 4 Assessment: Problem Set 0.1 + Viva 0.1 Mastery: 85 % (soft gate)

By the end of this unit you can…

  • State the probability-space triple \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)\) and define a portfolio loss as a random variable, rigorously enough to justify the "once-in-\(1/(1-q)\)-years" reading the Leitfaden attaches to its confidence levels.
  • Define Value-at-Risk as a quantile \( \mathrm{VaR}_q(L)=F_L^{-1}(q)\), and prove that for a (zero-mean) normal loss the VaR ratio between two confidence levels is just the ratio of standard-normal quantiles.
  • Reproduce the Leitfaden's CVA scalars \(0{,}71\) and \(1{,}33\) and the op-risk Problemfall factor \(5{,}0\,\%\) from first principles — and say exactly which inputs are regulatory and which are ÖRS modelling choices.
  • Derive the lognormal quantile from the normal, and recover the guide's own \(5{,}180\) and \(21{,}982\).
  • Implement every result in R and map each statistical input to where its data would come from.

Why start here, not with the balance sheet?

Everything quantitative in the Leitfaden is a statement about a quantile of a loss distribution: the IRB unexpected loss (a \(99{,}9\,\%\) conditional-default quantile), the parametric market-risk VaR, the CVA and op-risk scalings, the FLVaR. If the notion of "the loss not exceeded with probability \(q\)" is not airtight, none of those numbers are defensible. We make it airtight first, then spend the rest of the program applying it. The balance sheet / Deckungsmassen track (Unit 0.5) builds the other side of the comparison.
