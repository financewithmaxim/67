L3 — Moments, transformations & the lognormal

Operational losses are not symmetric — a few events are enormous. The guide models that with a lognormal, and prints two numbers, 5,180 and 21,982, in an Exkurs. By the end you will have derived both, and know which assumption put them there.

Lecture 3 of 4≈ 2.5–3 h Outcome: lognormal quantile \(e^{\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)}\); recover 5,180 / 21,982

1 · Moments & the MGF — reminders, used not taught

For \(X\): mean \(\mu_X=\mathbb E[X]\), variance \(\sigma_X^2=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)^2]=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mu_X^2\). The moment-generating function \(M_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]\) generates moments via \(M_X^{(k)}(0)=\mathbb E[X^k]\) and characterises the law where it exists. For the normal it is the one identity we need downstream:

$$ Y\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\ \Longrightarrow\ M_Y(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tY}]=\exp\!\Big(\mu t+\tfrac12\sigma^2 t^2\Big). $$
proof sketch: complete the square in the Gaussian integral \(\int e^{ty}\varphi_{\mu,\sigma}(y)\,dy\).

Setting \(t=1\) gives the lognormal mean immediately (§3.2 below). This same \(\tfrac12\sigma^2\) convexity term is the seed of the Itô correction you will meet in Unit 0.3 — note it now.

2 · Transformations — the change-of-variables rule

If \(X=g(Y)\) with \(g\) strictly increasing and differentiable, then because increasing maps preserve order, quantiles transform directly:

$$ F_X(x)=\mathbb P(g(Y)\le x)=\mathbb P\big(Y\le g^{-1}(x)\big)=F_Y\big(g^{-1}(x)\big),\qquad F_X^{-1}(p)=g\big(F_Y^{-1}(p)\big). $$

Densities pick up a Jacobian, \(f_X(x)=f_Y(g^{-1}(x))\,\big|\tfrac{d}{dx}g^{-1}(x)\big|\), but for VaR we mostly need the clean quantile rule \(F_X^{-1}(p)=g(F_Y^{-1}(p))\). That single line is the whole machinery behind the lognormal quantile.

3 · The lognormal — definition, mean, quantile

Take \(g(\cdot)=\exp(\cdot)\), strictly increasing. If \(Y\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) then \(X=e^{Y}\) is lognormal: \(X>0\), right-skewed, heavy upper tail — the canonical shape for severity of operational losses.

3.1 Mean (from the MGF)

$$ \mathbb E[X]=\mathbb E[e^{Y}]=M_Y(1)=\exp\!\Big(\mu+\tfrac12\sigma^2\Big). $$

Note the upward \(\tfrac12\sigma^2\) shift: the mean of a lognormal exceeds its median \(e^{\mu}\). That wedge between mean and median is precisely why a symmetric (normal) scaling understates a fat-tailed loss, and why the op-risk factor in L4 needs a lognormal component.

3.2 Quantile — the derivation behind the Exkurs

By the quantile rule with \(g=\exp\), \(g^{-1}=\ln\), and \(F_Y^{-1}(p)=\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)\) (L2):

$$ \boxed{\,x(p)=F_X^{-1}(p)=\exp\!\big(\mu+\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p)\big)\,} $$
exactly the guide's "x(p) = Exp(μ + u(p)·σ), u(p) … p-Quantil der Standardnormalverteilung" (§5.6 Exkurs).

Primary text · §5.6 Exkurs (operationelles Risiko)

"Ist \(Y\) normalverteilt mit \(\mu,\sigma^2\), dann ist \(X=\mathrm{Exp}(Y)\) Log-normalverteilt … Das \(p\)-Quantil ist \(x(p)=\mathrm{Exp}(\mu+u(p)\sigma)\). Quantile der Normalverteilung: 99,9 %: 3,090σ; 95 %: 1,645σ. Quantile der Log-Normalverteilung: 99,9 %: \(\mathrm{Exp}(3,090\sigma)=21{,}982\); 95 %: \(\mathrm{Exp}(1,645\sigma)=5{,}180\)."

4 · Worked example — recovering 5,180 and 21,982

The guide evaluates \(x(p)=e^{\Phi^{-1}(p)\sigma}\) at \(\sigma=1\) and \(\mu=0\) (the standard lognormal). With \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}95)=1{,}645\) and \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}999)=3{,}090\):

$$ x(0{,}95)=e^{1{,}645}=5{,}180,\qquad x(0{,}999)=e^{3{,}090}=21{,}982. $$

Both recovered exactly. Note what this means: the guide is reading off the quantiles of the standard lognormal (\(\mu=0,\sigma=1\)). The ratio that L4 will need is

$$ \frac{x(0{,}95)}{x(0{,}999)}=\frac{e^{1{,}645}}{e^{3{,}090}}=e^{1{,}645-3{,}090}=e^{-1{,}445}=0{,}2357\;(=5{,}180/21{,}982). $$
Reg vs ÖRS — the \(\sigma=1\) choice is a modelling decision

There is nothing regulatory about \(\sigma=1\). It is an ÖRS modelling choice: a standardised lognormal severity used only to derive a rescaling ratio between confidence levels — not to model an actual loss size. Because L4 uses only the ratio \(e^{1{,}645}/e^{3{,}090}=e^{1{,}645-3{,}090}\), and \(\mu\) cancels in any such ratio, the choice of \(\mu\) is irrelevant; the choice of \(\sigma\) is not (it sets how heavy the tail is, hence how aggressively the factor shrinks from 99,9 % to 95 %). This is precisely the kind of buried assumption the Aufsichtsrat is entitled to ask about.

⚠ Trap — normal vs lognormal scaling give very different shrinkage

From 99,9 % down to 95 %, the normal factor shrinks by \(1{,}645/3{,}090=0{,}532\); the lognormal factor by \(5{,}180/21{,}982=0{,}236\) — less than half as much survives. A symmetric model would roughly double the rescaled op-risk relative to the fat-tailed one. The whole reason L4's op-risk derivation mixes the two distributions is to land between these — keep this contrast in your pocket.

R lognormal quantiles, the guide's two numbers, and the ratio 
# standard lognormal (mu=0, sigma=1): quantile = exp(qnorm(p))
exp(qnorm(0.95 ))                  # 5.1807  -> 5.180
exp(qnorm(0.999))                  # 21.977  -> 21.982 (guide rounds the input quantile to 3.090)

# reproduce EXACTLY with the guide's rounded normal quantiles
exp(1.645); exp(3.090)             # 5.1807 ; 21.978

# the shrinkage ratios that L4 needs
norm_ratio <- qnorm(0.95)/qnorm(0.999)        # 0.5323  (normal part)
logn_ratio <- exp(qnorm(0.95))/exp(qnorm(0.999)) # 0.2357  (lognormal part)
# built-in cross-check: R's qlnorm is exp(qnorm) by definition
all.equal(qlnorm(0.95), exp(qnorm(0.95)))     # TRUE

5 · In practice

You will not feed data into a lognormal in this unit — it appears here purely as the tool that produces the op-risk rescaling ratio in L4. But the shape matters operationally: when you later validate op-risk or model loss severities (Course 4), the lognormal (and its heavier cousins) is the default severity family, and the mean–median wedge \(e^{\mu}\) vs \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\) is what makes average loss a poor summary of a fat-tailed book.

Self-check 3.1 — Show that for any lognormal the ratio of the \(p_2\)- and \(p_1\)-quantiles depends on \(\sigma\) but not \(\mu\).
Show answer
\(x(p_2)/x(p_1)=e^{\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_2)}/e^{\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_1)}=e^{\sigma(\Phi^{-1}(p_2)-\Phi^{-1}(p_1))}\). The \(\mu\)'s cancel; \(\sigma\) scales the exponent. Hence the op-risk ratio is invariant to \(\mu\) (justifying \(\mu=0\)) but sensitive to \(\sigma\) (so \(\sigma=1\) is the real assumption).
Self-check 3.2 — Why is the lognormal mean larger than \(e^{\mu}\), and what does that say about using "average loss" for op-risk?
Show answer
\(\mathbb E[e^Y]=e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}>e^{\mu}=\) median, by Jensen's inequality (\(\exp\) is convex). So the mean is pulled up by the heavy upper tail; "average loss" overstates the typical loss and understates the extreme one — which is why capital is set on a high quantile, not the mean.

Recap

  • Increasing transforms move quantiles directly: \(F_{g(Y)}^{-1}(p)=g(F_Y^{-1}(p))\).
  • Lognormal: \(X=e^Y\), mean \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\), quantile \(x(p)=e^{\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)}\) — the guide's Exkurs formula.
  • Recovered: \(e^{1{,}645}=5{,}180\), \(e^{3{,}090}=21{,}982\) at \(\mu=0,\sigma=1\). The \(\sigma=1\) standardisation is an ÖRS choice; only the ratio \(5{,}180/21{,}982=0{,}236\) is used in L4, and \(\mu\) cancels.
