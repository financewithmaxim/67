Module 23 — Liquidity deep dive: LCR, NSFR, ILAAP & the Leitfaden Liquidität

Capital absorbs losses; liquidity buys time. A solvent bank can still fail in an afternoon if it cannot meet its outflows, so the CRR bolts two ratios onto the capital edifice — the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR, kurzfristige Liquiditätsdeckungsquote) for a 30-day stress, and the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR, strukturelle Liquiditätsquote) for the one-year funding structure — and wraps both in a governance process, the ILAAP (Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process, interner Prozess zur Sicherstellung einer angemessenen Liquiditätsausstattung). This module derives both ratios from their cash-flow logic, builds an LCR by hand using the Annex I adjusted-amount waterfall that actually enforces the caps, tips it under 100%, places the ILAAP against the ICAAP, and ties the lot to the Raiffeisen IPS (Institutional Protection Scheme, institutsbezogenes Sicherungssystem) liquidity mechanism and the sector Leitfaden Liquidität.

By the end of this module you can…

  • Decompose the LCR into HQLA after haircuts, the Level 1 composition floor and the Level 2 caps over the 30-day stressed net cash outflow, and reproduce each building block from first principles.
  • Run the Annex I adjusted-amount waterfall and explain why the 40% and 15% caps bite on adjusted amounts, not on raw post-haircut totals.
  • Derive why the NSFR is a structural complement to the LCR rather than a duplicate, including the asymmetric derivative and interdependent-item treatments.
  • Build a numerical LCR from a stylised balance sheet — including secured funding and a downgrade-trigger outflow — and identify exactly which assumption tips it below 100%.
  • Place the ILAAP against the ICAAP and explain the ECB Guide's expectations on the survival period and the counterbalancing capacity.
  • Map the ALMM, the maturity ladder and intraday metrics onto the sector's IPS liquidity pooling and the inflow-cap exemptions, and tag each fixed factor as a regulatory value or an ÖRS modelling decision while stating the proportionality trade-off.

1 · Motivation — why this exists

Northern Rock was comfortably above its capital minimum the week its depositors queued on the pavement. Lehman's counterparties did not wait for an insolvency filing; they simply stopped rolling overnight funding, and a firm with positive equity ran out of cash. The lesson the Basel Committee drew is that solvency and liquidity are different risks: capital answers "are assets worth more than liabilities?", liquidity answers "can I pay what is due, today and over the coming year, when funding markets are hostile?". A bank can be solvent and dead.

The rest of this course is about capital adequacy — the capital ratios of Module 3, IRB parameters, the output floor. This module is its liquidity twin. Two ratios carry the load. The LCR asks whether a high-quality liquid buffer survives a sharp 30-day stress; the NSFR asks whether the funding structure is stable enough over a year that the bank is not perpetually rolling short money into long assets. Around them sits a governance and monitoring layer — the ALMM (Additional Liquidity Monitoring Metrics, zusätzliche Liquiditätsüberwachungskennzahlen), the maturity ladder, intraday metrics — and an internal self-assessment, the ILAAP, which is to liquidity what the ICAAP (covered in Module 22) is to capital.

▶ The one-sentence version

The LCR is a stock requirement (enough liquid assets for a 30-day siege); the NSFR is a structural requirement (don't fund a mortgage book with overnight repo). You can pass one and fail the other, which is exactly why both exist.

2 · Concept & logic

2.1 The LCR cash-flow identity

The LCR (governed by Delegated Regulation (EU) 2015/61, the LCR-DelVO) imagines a single, acute, idiosyncratic-plus-market 30-day stress: a partial deposit run, loss of wholesale funding, rating-trigger collateral calls, drawdowns on committed lines, and the inability to roll secured funding except against the very best collateral. The bank must hold enough unencumbered, genuinely liquid assets — the HQLA (High-Quality Liquid Assets, hochliquide Aktiva) — to cover the net cash that flows out during those 30 days.

$$ \text{LCR} = \frac{\text{Liquidity buffer (HQLA after haircuts, floor and caps)}}{\text{NCO}_{30}} \;\ge\; 100\% $$
where \( \text{NCO}_{30} \) is the 30-day stressed net cash outflow (outflows minus capped inflows), and the buffer is the stock of liquid assets after asset-class haircuts, the Level 1 composition floor and the Level 2 caps enforced via the Annex I adjusted-amount algorithm of §3.1.

The \(\ge 100\%\) minimum is the regulatory anchor regulatory value: the buffer must fully cover the modelled net drain. There is no partial credit — at 99% you breach.

2.2 The NSFR structural identity

The NSFR lives in CRR Part Six, Title IV (Articles 428a ff., binding since June 2021). Where the LCR is a 30-day cash test, the NSFR is a one-year balance-sheet-structure test. It weights every liability by how stable a funding source it is — the ASF (Available Stable Funding, verfügbare stabile Refinanzierung) — and every asset by how much stable funding it requires because it cannot be quickly turned into cash — the RSF (Required Stable Funding, erforderliche stabile Refinanzierung).

$$ \text{NSFR} = \frac{\text{ASF}}{\text{RSF}} = \frac{\sum_i \alpha_i \, L_i}{\sum_j \beta_j \, A_j} \;\ge\; 100\% $$
where \(L_i\) are liability/equity items with ASF factors \(\alpha_i \in [0,1]\), \(A_j\) are asset and off-balance items with RSF factors \(\beta_j \in [0,1]\); capital and long (>1y) funding score high \(\alpha\), illiquid long assets score high \(\beta\). ASF is the numerator and RSF is the denominator — each is a weighted sum, not a term inside the fraction.
▶ Why two ratios, not one

Imagine a bank that holds a vast government-bond buffer (LCR sails through) but funds it entirely with 1-month wholesale paper. The LCR is blind to what happens on day 31, when that paper matures and the market is still shut. The NSFR catches precisely this: short funding of any non-trivially-liquid asset costs RSF that the 1-month funding (0% ASF) cannot supply. The LCR is the tactical 30-day test; the NSFR is the strategic term-funding test. Neither subsumes the other.

3 · Derivation

3.1 Building the buffer: levels, haircuts, the composition floor, and the cap waterfall

Not all liquid assets are equally liquid in a fire sale, so the buffer is tiered, and each tier is haircut and constrained. Working from the LCR-DelVO logic:

HQLA tierTypical assetsHaircutComposition constraint
Level 1 (non-covered-bond)Coins/banknotes, central-bank reserves (withdrawable in stress), EU sovereign & certain supranational bonds (0% risk weight)0%must be ≥ 30% of the buffer (composition floor)
Level 1 covered bonds (extremely-high-quality, Art. 10(1)(f))EHQ covered bonds meeting the strict criteria7% minimumeffectively ≤ 70% of the buffer (the mirror of the 30% non-covered floor)
Level 2ACertain ≥AA- corporate bonds, high-quality covered bonds, some regional/PSE exposures15%total L2 ≤ 40% of the buffer; within that, L2B ≤ 15%
Level 2BLower-rated corporate bonds, qualifying RMBS, certain equities in a major index25%–50% by sub-class (e.g. RMBS 25%, equities 50%)

The haircuts regulatory value are a stylised model of the price you would actually realise selling into a stressed market; the floor and caps regulatory value stop a bank from claiming a "liquid" buffer that is mostly lower-grade or single-asset-class paper. Level 1 is not uniformly uncapped: the genuinely uncapped, 0%-haircut core is non-covered-bond Level 1, and the buffer must be at least 30% of it. Level 1 EHQ covered bonds carry the 7% haircut and, via that same 30% floor, are effectively capped at 70% of the buffer.

The Annex I adjusted-amount waterfall — what actually enforces the caps

Here is the single most error-prone part of an LCR build, and the one a defender of the methodology must get right. The 40% and 15% caps do not bite as a flat percentage of the raw post-haircut total. They bite on adjusted amounts: the DelVO Annex I prescribes a stepwise unwinding algorithm that asks "what would the buffer composition be if every short-term secured funding / lending / collateral-swap transaction collateralised with HQLA were unwound back to cash?". The caps are then applied to those unwound (adjusted) amounts, and the binding cap is the one that produces the smallest buffer.

Operationally, with post-haircut adjusted amounts \(V_1\) (non-covered-bond Level 1), \(V_{cb}\) (Level 1 covered), \(V_{2A}\), \(V_{2B}\):

$$ \text{Buffer} = \big(V_1 + V_{cb} + V_{2A} + V_{2B}\big) - A_{cb} - A_{2A} - A_{2B} $$
where the three "excess" claw-backs \(A\) are computed in sequence from the adjusted amounts: \(A_{2B}\) removes Level 2B above the 15% adjusted sub-cap, \(A_{2A}\) removes total Level 2 above the 40% adjusted cap, and \(A_{cb}\) removes Level 1 covered bonds (and, in the algorithm, any L2) needed to keep non-covered Level 1 at ≥ 30% of the adjusted buffer. The adjusted-amount tests are equivalent to the closed-form 15/85 and 2/3 ratios only when no secured-transaction unwinding changes the composition; in a real book with repos against HQLA, you must run the algorithm.

The practical reading of the two ratio forms the algorithm reduces to: total Level 2 may not exceed \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) of the adjusted Level 1 amount (so that L2 ≤ 40% of the whole), and L2B may not exceed \(\tfrac{15}{85}\) of "Level 1 plus capped Level 2A" (so that L2B ≤ 15% of the whole). When a cap binds, the excess is excluded from the buffer entirely, not merely down-weighted.

⚠ Caps on adjusted amounts, never on the raw total

The seductive shortcut — "L2B sub-cap = 15% × total post-haircut HQLA" — is wrong. It only happens to give the right answer when no cap binds; the moment a repo against your sovereign bonds, or a covered-bond-heavy buffer, shifts the composition, the shortcut over- or under-states the eligible buffer. Always compute the adjusted amounts first, then apply 30% / 40% / 15% to those.

3.2 Building the 30-day net cash outflow

The denominator is built bottom-up from run-off rates applied to each liability type and inflow rates applied to contractual receipts, with a crucial asymmetry: inflows are capped.

$$ \text{NCO}_{30} = \underbrace{\sum_k r_k \, O_k}_{\text{outflows}} \;-\; \min\!\Big(\underbrace{\sum_m i_m \, I_m}_{\text{inflows}},\; c \cdot \sum_k r_k\,O_k\Big) $$
where \(r_k\) is the run-off (outflow) rate on liability/commitment \(O_k\), \(i_m\) the inflow rate on receivable \(I_m\), and \(c\) the inflow cap, normally 75% of gross outflows. The cap can be 90% for specialised/promotional-lending institutions or 0% exempt for fully pass-through intragroup/IPS and promotional flows — see §5.

The run-off rates encode behavioural assumptions about who flees first:

Funding / commitment typeRun-off / inflow rateLogic
Stable retail deposits (DGS-covered, transactional)5% (a 3% reduced rate exists for certain DGS-protected very-stable deposits)insured, sticky households
Less-stable retail deposits10% and uphigh-balance, rate-chasing, non-resident
Operational deposits — DGS-covered portion5%insured and operationally tied — doubly sticky
Operational deposits — excess / non-covered portion (Art. 27)25%operationally tied but uninsured
Non-operational deposits, non-financial corporates40% (lower if DGS-covered)managed treasury cash, flighty
Unsecured wholesale from financial institutions100%first to run; assume full loss
Secured funding (repo) — by quality of collateral pledgedL1 collateral 0% roll-off; L2A 15%; L2B 25–50%; non-HQLA up to 100%only the best collateral keeps rolling in stress
Derivatives & collateral: rating-downgrade trigger add-on (Art. 30)full collateral/contractual outflow on a 3-notch downgrade; plus net derivative outflows and a market-value-change add-ondowngrade calls and margin moves bite immediately

Every one of these rates is a regulatory value: the calibration is hard-wired by the DelVO precisely so that the LCR is comparable across banks. The bank's only modelling freedom is classification — is this deposit "stable", is this portion "operational", is this collateral "Level 1"? That classification is where supervisory scrutiny concentrates.

▶ Reg point — secured funding and derivatives dominate real LCRs

For a treasury-active bank, the largest denominator lines are usually secured funding (repo books) and derivative flows, not retail deposits. A repo against Level 1 collateral rolls at 0% — the lender keeps lending against the best paper — but the same cash secured against lower-grade collateral runs off at the collateral's HQLA haircut and beyond. The downgrade-trigger add-on of Art. 30 captures contractual collateral the bank must post on a downgrade of up to 3 notches. Omit these and your LCR is structurally wrong, not merely imprecise.

3.3 The NSFR factors, derived — and its three asymmetries

ASF and RSF factors are the structural mirror of run-off rates. A funding source that will still be there in a year gets a high ASF; an asset that cannot be liquidated within a year needs a high RSF.

ItemFactorReading
ASF: regulatory capital, liabilities >1y residual100%fully stable funding
ASF: stable retail/SME deposits <1y95%sticky even though short
ASF: less-stable retail <1y90%still mostly there
ASF: non-financial corporate <1y50%half assumed to persist
ASF: funding from financial institutions <6m0%no structural stability
RSF: cash, central-bank reserves, Level-1 HQLA0%–5%needs almost no stable funding
RSF: performing residential mortgages (≤35% RW)65%long, illiquid, but good quality
RSF: other performing loans, NFCs, ≥1y85%needs near-full term funding
RSF: encumbered assets >1y, other illiquid100%fully tied up
RSF add-on: 20% of gross derivative liabilities20%captures potential future margin/replacement needs

Three asymmetries make the NSFR description structurally complete, and a defender must name them:

  • Asset vs. liability maturity asymmetry. On the liability (ASF) side, a sub-one-year residual maturity already costs you stable funding; on the asset (RSF) side, the thresholds and factors are set so that the same nominal tenor is treated more conservatively for assets than the symmetric mirror would imply — the regulation deliberately tilts toward holding more stable funding than a naive mirror would require.
  • Interdependent assets and liabilities. Where supervisors recognise a strictly matched pass-through structure (e.g. certain promotional/centralised regulated savings, or directly funded leasing), the linked asset and liability can be assigned 0% RSF and 0% ASF respectively, so the pair neither demands nor supplies stable funding. This matters in an IPS where central-institution funding is contractually matched to member assets.
  • Derivatives: net plus a gross add-on. Derivative assets and liabilities are netted within a set of rules; on top of the net figure, a flat 20% RSF is charged on gross derivative liabilities, recognising that the net position understates the stable funding a derivative book can suddenly require.

Notice the core structural argument: a 1-month interbank deposit (0% ASF) funding a mortgage (65% RSF) makes NSFR < 100% by construction, even though that same mortgage barely shows up in the LCR's 30-day window. That is the NSFR doing the job the LCR cannot.

4 · Worked example — build an LCR and tip it

▶ Stylised Raiffeisenbank balance sheet (€m)

Liquid assets (market value): central-bank reserves €120; EU sovereign bonds (non-covered L1) €80; AA covered bonds (L2A) €60; qualifying RMBS (L2B) €40.
Funding (30-day relevant): stable retail deposits €1,500; less-stable retail €300; non-operational NFC deposits €250; unsecured FI funding €100; repo financing secured against the EU sovereign bonds €70; undrawn committed corporate lines €200.
Derivatives: a one-notch-within-3 downgrade clause would force €15 of additional collateral.
Inflows (next 30 days): performing retail loan repayments €60; interbank receivables from FIs €40.

4.1 Numerator — the buffer after haircuts, floor and the cap waterfall

Apply haircuts to the adjusted amounts. Here the €70 repo is secured against Level 1 collateral that is already in the buffer; the Annex I unwinding leaves Level 1 unchanged because the pledged bonds and the secured cash net out in the adjusted-amount step (the bonds remain encumbered for haircut purposes but the example assumes they are reported unencumbered for buffer eligibility — verify the encumbrance treatment in practice). Post-haircut adjusted amounts:

Non-covered L1 = 120 + 80 = 200 (0%). L2A = 60 × (1 − 0.15) = 51. L2B (RMBS, 25% haircut) = 40 × (1 − 0.25) = 30. No Level 1 covered bonds here, so the 7% line and 70% sub-limit are not engaged.

Composition floor. Non-covered L1 must be ≥ 30% of the buffer. Provisional buffer = 200 + 51 + 30 = 281; non-covered L1 share = 200/281 ≈ 71% ≥ 30% — floor satisfied.

Level 2 caps on adjusted amounts. Adjusted L2 total = 51 + 30 = 81. The 40% cap is enforced as L2 ≤ \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) × adjusted L1 = \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) × 200 = 133.3 — not binding (81 < 133.3). The L2B sub-cap is enforced as L2B ≤ \(\tfrac{15}{85}\) × (adjusted L1 + capped L2A) = \(\tfrac{15}{85}\) × (200 + 51) = 0.17647 × 251 = 44.3 — not binding (30 < 44.3). Note this is computed from the adjusted amounts via the 15/85 ratio, not as 15% of the raw 281; the two coincide here only because no cap binds.

$$ \text{Buffer} = 200 + 51 + 30 = 281 \text{ €m} $$

4.2 Denominator — stressed net cash outflow

Gross outflows (illustrative rates — verify):

ItemAmountRateOutflow
Stable retail1,5005%75.0
Less-stable retail30010%30.0
Non-operational NFC25040%100.0
Unsecured FI funding100100%100.0
Repo vs. L1 collateral700%0.0
Downgrade-trigger collateral (Art. 30)15100%15.0
Undrawn corporate lines20010%20.0

The repo against Level 1 collateral rolls at 0% — the lender keeps the funding open against the best paper — so it adds nothing to the drain even though it is €70 of secured funding. Had the same cash been secured against the RMBS, it would run off at the L2B rate. Inflows: retail 60 × 50% = 30; FI 40 × 100% = 40; gross inflows = 70. Inflow cap = 75% × 340 = 255, so the full 70 is admitted.

$$ \text{NCO}_{30} = 340 - \min(70,\;255) = 340 - 70 = 270 \text{ €m} $$
$$ \text{LCR} = \frac{281}{270} = 104.1\% \;\ge\; 100\% \;\checkmark $$

4.3 What tips it below 100%

⚠ The classification cliff

Suppose supervision rejects the "stable" tag on €600 of the retail book — those accounts pay a promotional rate and are not transactional — reclassifying them as less-stable. That moves €600 from 5% to 10%: outflows rise by 600 × (10% − 5%) = €30. New gross outflows = 370, NCO = 370 − 70 = 300, and LCR = 281 / 300 = 93.7% — a breach, with not one cent of the balance sheet changed. The buffer didn't move; a behavioural classification did. This is why the ILAAP narrative around deposit stability, and the documentation behind it, is examined as hard as the number.

A second tipping route runs through the downgrade trigger we now model explicitly. A one-notch downgrade can simultaneously enlarge the Art. 30 collateral outflow, make the RMBS ineligible as L2B (shrinking the numerator) and turn the L1-collateral repo into a partial run-off (the lender re-margins). Numerator falls and denominator rises through the same event. The LCR is non-linear in stress precisely because numerator and denominator are correlated through one downgrade — which is exactly why the survival period in §5.2 is read off a multi-period ladder, not a single ratio.

5 · In practice — the sector

5.1 The IPS liquidity mechanism and the inflow-cap exemptions

The Raiffeisen sector is an IPS under CRR Article 113(7), and liquidity is one of its load-bearing functions. Member banks pool liquidity: a central institution operates a sector liquidity-management arrangement so that a local Raiffeisenbank with a temporary outflow can draw on sector resources rather than fire-selling assets. Several regulatory consequences follow.

First, intra-IPS exposures can be exempt from the unsecured-FI 100% run-off and may receive a preferential (often 0% / 25% symmetric) treatment regulatory value — but only because the IPS contract makes that funding genuinely available in stress; the FMA/ECB will test that. Second, the inflow cap is not a flat 75%: monies from an IPS or intragroup arrangement that are a fully committed liquidity line, and certain pass-through flows, can be exempt from the cap (so they reduce NCO beyond the 75% ceiling), and specialised/promotional lenders may use the 90% cap rather than 75%. For an IPS sector this exemption is not a footnote — it is the mechanism by which centrally pooled liquidity is recognised. Third, the sector typically monitors a consolidated or aggregated liquidity position alongside each member's solo LCR.

▶ ÖRS modelling decision — sector deposit-stability calibration

The DelVO fixes the run-off rates, but classification of the deposit base into stable / less-stable / operational (and the DGS-covered vs. excess split that drives the 5% vs. 25% operational rate) is where the sector adds value. The Leitfaden Liquidität codifies a consistent, conservative classification taxonomy and the data feeds (account flags, transactionality tests, DGS-coverage mapping) used across all member institutions ÖRS modelling decision. The trade-off: a uniform sector taxonomy buys comparability, central-function efficiency and defensibility before the Aufsichtsrat, but it can be more conservative than a tailored local model would be for an atypical member, costing some buffer headroom. Proportionality means smaller members accept the standard taxonomy rather than build bespoke behavioural models.

5.2 ALMM, the maturity ladder, intraday, survival period

The point-in-time LCR is necessary but not sufficient, so supervisors require the ALMM (Additional Liquidity Monitoring Metrics, set out in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2016/313 amending (EU) 680/2014): the concentration of funding by counterparty and product, the prices for various funding tenors, roll-over behaviour, and the maturity ladder (Laufzeitenband) — a time-bucketed contractual cash-flow gap report extended beyond 30 days. The maturity ladder is where the bank reads its survival period (Überlebenshorizont): the longest horizon over which the counterbalancing capacity (Liquiditätspuffer plus credible actions) covers the cumulative net gap under stress. The ECB Guide to the ILAAP expects the survival period to be an explicit, board-owned metric, not a by-product.

Intraday liquidity (Innertagesliquidität) is a separate axis the LCR ignores entirely: even a bank that survives the 30-day test can fail to settle a payment at 11:00 if its intraday position in a payment system runs dry. The monitoring tools (daily maximum liquidity requirement, available intraday liquidity, total payments) are operational and granular.

5.3 ILAAP vs ICAAP

The ILAAP is the liquidity counterpart of the ICAAP (Module 22). Both are internal, board-owned adequacy processes; both feed the SREP (Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process), where a weak ILAAP can produce Pillar 2 liquidity guidance or quantitative add-ons.

DimensionICAAPILAAP
Resourcecapital (loss absorption)liquidity & funding (payment capacity)

The interplay matters: a liquidity stress that forces asset fire-sales crystallises losses and so consumes capital — the ILAAP's adverse scenarios should be consistent with the ICAAP's, and vice versa. They are two readings of one stressed balance sheet.

6 · Nuances & traps

⚠ The buffer must be usable, not just present

HQLA is only liquid if it is unencumbered and you are willing to use it. A bank that treats its LCR buffer as untouchable defeats the purpose: the buffer is meant to be drawn down in stress, dipping below 100% temporarily with a supervisory plan to rebuild. Counting encumbered collateral (including bonds pledged in your repo book), or assets you would never actually sell because the loss would breach a capital ratio, is a classic self-deception.

⚠ Level 1 is not "uncapped and 0%"

The clean story — "Level 1 is uncapped and zero-haircut" — is only true of non-covered-bond Level 1. EHQ covered bonds in Level 1 carry a 7% haircut, and the 30% non-covered-bond composition floor effectively caps L1 covered bonds at 70% of the buffer. A covered-bond-heavy Austrian buffer can hit this floor well before it hits the 40% Level 2 cap.

⚠ Currency mismatch hides inside a healthy total

A consolidated LCR of 130% can mask a CHF or USD sub-portfolio at 60%. The DelVO requires monitoring of significant currencies separately, because in a stress you cannot pay a Swiss-franc outflow with euro bonds without an FX market that may itself be frozen. For an Austrian bank with a legacy CHF mortgage tail this is not academic.

⚠ Don't double-count with IRRBB or the FLVaR

The maturity ladder looks like the repricing gap of Module 6 (IRRBB) and the cash-flow projections of Module 9 (FLVaR, Mischzinssatz), but they answer different questions: IRRBB asks how earnings/economic value move when rates move; the liquidity ladder asks whether cash is there regardless of price. A position can be interest-rate-hedged and still illiquid. Reuse the cash-flow plumbing, not the conclusions.

▶ Reg point — minima, phase-ins and the cutoff date

The LCR minimum is 100% and the NSFR minimum is 100%; the LCR was phased in (60% → 100% over 2015–2018) and the binding NSFR applies from June 2021. Always re-confirm the current DelVO and CRR consolidated text — liquidity calibrations have been amended (e.g. covered-bond and securitisation treatments) and any figure post-dating the 09/2024 Leitfaden must be checked against EUR-Lex/EBA/FMA before it goes to the Aufsichtsrat.

⚠ The inflow cap is a behavioural floor on the buffer — except where exempt

Banks try to manufacture inflows (e.g. very short interbank lending) to shrink NCO. The 75% inflow cap deliberately defeats this: at least 25% of gross outflows must be met by HQLA, not by counting on incoming cash that may not arrive in a stress. But the cap is not universal — pass-through intragroup/IPS liquidity and promotional flows can be exempt, and specialised lenders may use the 90% cap. Know which exemption your IPS relies on before you assume a flat 75%.

▶ Recap

LCR = Stock-Test über 30 Tage (Liquiditätspuffer ÷ Nettomittelabfluss ≥ 100%); die Caps wirken auf angepasste Beträge (Anhang-I-Wasserfall), nicht auf den Roh-Bruttowert. NSFR = struktureller Ein-Jahres-Test (ASF ÷ RSF ≥ 100%) mit asymmetrischer Aktiv-/Passivbehandlung, verbundenen Posten und Derivate-Aufschlag. Klassifikation der Einlagen, nicht nur der Puffer, treibt die Quote. ILAAP ist das Liquiditätspendant zum ICAAP; im Sektor bündelt das IPS Liquidität, der Leitfaden Liquidität vereinheitlicht die Kalibrierung.

Recap

  • The LCR = the liquidity buffer (HQLA after haircuts, the 30% non-covered-bond Level 1 composition floor and the Level 2 caps) over the 30-day stressed net cash outflow, and must be ≥ 100%.
  • The 40% and 15% caps are enforced on the Annex I adjusted amounts (after unwinding secured transactions), not as a flat percentage of the raw post-haircut total — the shortcut gives the wrong answer whenever a cap or the composition floor binds.
  • Level 1 is not uniformly uncapped: non-covered-bond L1 is the 0%-haircut, uncapped core, but EHQ covered bonds carry a ~7% haircut and are effectively capped at ~70% of the buffer via the 30% floor.
  • The NCO uses regulatory run-off rates including secured-funding (repo) roll-off by collateral quality and Art. 30 downgrade-trigger collateral outflows, minus inflows capped at 75% (90% for specialised lenders, with IPS/pass-through exemptions).
  • The NSFR (ASF ÷ RSF ≥ 100%) is a structural one-year complement with three asymmetries: conservative asset-vs-liability maturity treatment, 0%/0% interdependent assets/liabilities, and a 20% RSF add-on on gross derivative liabilities.
  • A pure deposit-classification change tipped the worked LCR into breach with no change in assets; the ILAAP, survival period, ALMM, maturity ladder and intraday metrics extend the picture, and the IPS pools liquidity under a sector-standardised taxonomy.
🇦🇹 Zusammenfassung (Deutsch)
  • Die LCR (Liquiditätsdeckungsquote) = Liquiditätspuffer (HQLA nach Abschlägen, 30%-Stufe-1-Untergrenze und Caps) geteilt durch den 30-Tage-Stress-Nettomittelabfluss; Mindestwert 100%.
  • Die Caps (Stufe 2 max. 40%, Stufe 2B max. 15%) wirken auf die angepassten Beträge des Anhang-I-Wasserfalls, nicht auf den Roh-Bruttowert.
  • Stufe 1 ist nicht durchgehend ungedeckelt: nur Nicht-Covered-Bond-Stufe-1 ist der 0%-Abschlag-Kern; EHQ-Pfandbriefe tragen 7% und sind über die 30%-Untergrenze faktisch auf 70% begrenzt.
  • Der Nettomittelabfluss umfasst auch besicherte Refinanzierung (Repo, Abfluss je nach Sicherheitenqualität) und Rating-Trigger-Abflüsse (Art. 30); Zuflüsse sind bei 75% gedeckelt (Ausnahmen: IPS/Durchleitung, 90% für Förderbanken).
  • Die NSFR (ASF ÷ RSF ≥ 100%) ist die strukturelle Ein-Jahres-Ergänzung mit asymmetrischer Aktiv-/Passivbehandlung, verbundenen Posten (0%/0%) und einem 20%-RSF-Aufschlag auf Brutto-Derivateverbindlichkeiten.
  • Der ILAAP ist das Liquiditätspendant zum ICAAP und fließt in den SREP ein; im Sektor bündelt das IPS die Liquidität und der Leitfaden Liquidität vereinheitlicht die Einlagenklassifikation — Vergleichbarkeit gegen lokale Feinsteuerung abgewogen.
