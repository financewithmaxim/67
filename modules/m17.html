Module 17 — Einlagensicherung: DGSD, ESAEG, and how the DGS interacts with the IPS and early intervention

The Einlagensicherung (deposit guarantee) is the post-failure backstop of the system: distinct from capital (which absorbs loss) and from the IPS (which prevents failure). This module places the EU DGSD, the Austrian ESAEG and the sector's R-IPS on one map, and shows why the Früherkennung's early-warning output is exactly what lets the sector act before a depositor payout is ever needed.

⚖️ Verify against current sources (FMA / ESA / EUR-Lex)

This is the lowest-priority, verify-heavy module of the course, and deposit-guarantee specifics are the most likely to have moved since this material was authored — and they may post-date the Leitfaden (09/2024) itself. Treat every figure, deadline and scheme name below as "as of the framework — verify current status". In particular you MUST check against primary sources before relying on any of it:

  • the current Austrian scheme structure (number of statutory schemes, who belongs to which) — verify on the FMA register;
  • the current Raiffeisen deposit-guarantee arrangement (which scheme the sector's banks are members of today) — verify on the FMA register, not from memory;
  • coverage, payout deadlines and the target level — verify against the DGSD on EUR-Lex, the ESAEG in the RIS, and EBA/ESA guidance;
  • any 2024–2026 changes (e.g. the EU's Crisis Management and Deposit Insurance / CMDI review may alter payout, scope, or the use of DGS funds).

What is stable (and what the exam tests) is the conceptual architecture: DGS = compensation after failure; IPS = prevention before failure; covered deposits = super-preferred. The exact numbers are secondary here.

By the end of this module you can…

  • Explain why a deposit guarantee exists — bank-run dynamics (link to M1) — and why it is a post-failure backstop, not capital and not the IPS.
  • State the core DGSD (2014/49/EU) parameters (coverage, payout deadline, target level, ex-ante risk-based funding) and the Austrian ESAEG transposition (covered deposits, Anlegerentschädigung).
  • Draw the crucial IPS (Art. 113(7) CRR) vs DGS (DGSD/ESAEG) distinction — prevention vs compensation — and explain when an IPS-run DGS may use its funds for alternative (failure-prevention) measures.
  • Locate the DGS/IPS in the BRRD/SRM creditor hierarchy (covered deposits super-preferred) and show how the RTFA + Ampelung feed the sector's decision to deploy IPS support before a payout.
  • Reason about funding & moral hazard: risk-based contributions, the 0,8% target, and why ex-ante funding + prevention reduce the chance of ever paying out.

1 · Motivation — why a deposit guarantee exists

Recall the fragility from Module 1: a bank funds illiquid, long-dated assets with liquid, on-demand deposits. A depositor who fears the bank may fail has a private incentive to withdraw first, before the cash runs out — and because everyone reasons the same way, the fear is self-fulfilling. This is a classic coordination failure: a Bankrun (bank run) can sink even a fundamentally solvent bank purely through illiquidity.

The deposit guarantee breaks the coordination failure at its root. If a small depositor knows their money is protected up to a credible limit regardless of what others do, they have no reason to run — and the run does not start. So the guarantee's first job is confidence: it works precisely when it is not used, by removing the incentive that triggers runs.

▶ Three different jobs, three different tools

Do not conflate the layers of protection — the exam loves this:

  • Kapital / Eigenmittel (capital / own funds) — absorbs loss inside the bank. First line. (Modules 1–2.)
  • IPS (Institutssicherungssystem / institutional protection scheme) — prevents failure by supporting a member before it fails. Pre-emptive. The Früherkennung serves this layer.
  • DGS / Einlagensicherung (deposit-guarantee scheme) — compensates depositors after failure. The last-resort backstop, capped per depositor.

Capital and the IPS try to make sure the DGS never has to pay. The DGS exists for the case where they fail.

2 · Concept & logic — DGSD and ESAEG

2.1 The EU layer — DGSD (Directive 2014/49/EU)

The Deposit Guarantee Schemes Directive harmonises deposit protection across the EU. As a Directive it is not directly applicable — it must be transposed into national law (contrast the directly-binding CRR; see M1). Its core parameters, as of the framework — verify current status:

ParameterValue (as of framework — verify)Nature
Coverage level (Deckungssumme)€100.000 per depositor per institutionregulatory harmonised DGSD ceiling. Not per account.
Payout deadline (Auszahlungsfrist)shortening toward 7 working daysregulatory verify current deadlines/targets — the phase-in to 7 working days post-dates the original directive.
Target level (Zielausstattung)0,8% of covered depositsregulatory minimum of available financial means, reached by ex-ante contributions.
Contributions (Beiträge)risk-based, ex-anteregulatory riskier banks pay more (EBA methodology).
Temporary high balancesextra cover, time-limitedregulatory e.g. proceeds from a house sale — covered above €100k for a limited window. verify scope/limit

Note the target is 0,8% of covered deposits — the protected subset (deposits within scope and within the ceiling), not total deposits and not total liabilities. That denominator matters: it ties the fund's size to exactly the exposure it insures.

2.2 The Austrian layer — ESAEG

Austria transposes the DGSD via the ESAEG — Einlagensicherungs- und Anlegerentschädigungsgesetz (Deposit Guarantee and Investor Compensation Act). It defines what counts as a gedeckte Einlage (covered deposit), the contribution mechanics, and bundles in Anlegerentschädigung (investor compensation, for claims arising from investment services) up to €20.000 regulatory — a separate, lower cap from the €100.000 deposit cover.

⚖️ Austrian scheme structure — VERIFY on the FMA register

As of the framework: since 2019 Austria consolidated into a single statutory scheme for most banks — Einlagensicherung AUSTRIA Ges.m.b.H. Before that, the Raiffeisen sector ran its own scheme, the Österreichische Raiffeisen-Einlagensicherung.

The current Raiffeisen deposit-guarantee membership/structure is to be VERIFIED against the FMA's current register — do not assert it from memory. Distinguish two roles that are easy to blur:

  • the ÖRS — Österreichische Raiffeisen-Sicherungseinrichtung — is the sector's Sicherungseinrichtung (protection body) named in the Leitfaden; it operates the R-IPS and runs the Früherkennung over its members;
  • which statutory DGS the sector's banks belong to (the single AUSTRIA scheme vs a sector arrangement) is a separate question of deposit-guarantee membership — check FMA's current register.

The Leitfaden uses the ÖRS as the body running the early-warning system; the deposit-guarantee scheme membership is the thing most likely to have changed.

3 · The crucial distinction — IPS vs DGS

If you remember one thing from this module, remember this table. The R-IPS and the DGS are two different machines with two different jobs and two different legal bases.

R-IPS (Institutssicherungssystem)DGS (Einlagensicherung)
Legal basisArt. 113(7) CRR regulatoryDGSD 2014/49/EU → ESAEG regulatory
FunctionPrevention — keep members solvent & liquidCompensation — pay out depositors
TimingBEFORE failure (pre-emptive)AFTER failure (deposits unavailable / resolution)
TriggerMonitoring signals — the Früherkennung! (Ampelung, RTFA)Feststellung der Nichtverfügbarkeit (deposits declared unavailable) or resolution
Beneficiarythe member institutionthe depositor
Coverage / benefitmutual support; 0% intra-IPS risk weight + waiver of large-exposure limits intra-IPS€100.000 per depositor per institution
Fundingby the members (sector-funded)risk-based ex-ante contributions to 0,8% target

The IPS benefits are themselves a powerful incentive: an Art. 113(7) IPS lets members assign a 0% risk weight to intra-IPS exposures and waive the large-exposure (Großkredit) limits for them — in exchange for joint monitoring and a binding obligation to support each other. That monitoring obligation is the regulatory home of the Früherkennung.

3.1 The linkage — when an IPS-run DGS can prevent failure

Here is the conceptual bridge that makes this module matter. An IPS that is also officially recognised as (or operates) a DGS is permitted, under conditions, to use its available financial means not only to pay out depositors but for failure-prevention measures — the DGSD's "alternative measures". Instead of waiting for the bank to fail and then compensating depositors (the costly path), the scheme can deploy funds early to keep the bank alive, provided this is cheaper than a payout (the least-cost / cheaper-than-payout test) and the conditions are met.

Why the Früherkennung is the enabler

Early action requires an early signal. You cannot deploy preventive support against a failure you only discover at the counter. The Früherkennung's output — the RTFA utilisation and the Ampelung (traffic lights, Module 4) — is precisely the forward-looking trigger that lets the sector act while options are still cheap. RED in the Extremfall is not the end of the story; in this system it is the start of the IPS's preventive intervention. The early-warning system is what converts "compensate after" into "prevent before".

4 · Worked example

Worked example — RED in the Extremfall: IPS prevention vs DGS payout

A sector member bank — call it RB-Süd — has covered deposits of, say, €800m and is steered through the Früherkennung.

The IPS path (what actually happens):

  1. The quarterly RTFA shows the Extremfall utilisation crossing into RED (>95%; Module 4) — economic substance no longer covers the 99,9% risk.
  2. The ÖRS / Sektorrisikokomitee (SRK) reviews the case: the detailed RED report (concrete steps & timing — Module 11) lands on the committee's desk.
  3. The IPS deploys preventive measures — capital and/or liquidity support — to restore RB-Süd's solvency and liquidity before any deposit becomes unavailable.
  4. RB-Süd does not fail. No Feststellung der Nichtverfügbarkeit is ever made. The DGS payout is never triggered. Depositor loss = €0.

The counterfactual (no IPS):

  1. Absent preventive support, RB-Süd fails; deposits are declared unavailable.
  2. The DGS must pay out €100.000 per depositor per institution, within the (shortening) deadline — drawing on the ex-ante fund (0,8% target).
  3. The DGS then becomes a creditor of the Insolvenzmasse (the estate) and recovers what it can — but covered-deposit claims are super-preferred (see §5), and recovery is slow and partial.
  4. Depositors above €100k take losses on the excess; the sector's confidence takes a hit.

The lesson: the IPS intervention is the cheaper, earlier intervention — and the Früherkennung is what makes it possible by surfacing the problem while it is still fixable. The DGS payout is the expensive fallback you are trying to never reach.

5 · In practice — interaction with early intervention & resolution

The DGS and IPS do not live in a vacuum; they sit alongside the EU recovery-and-resolution machinery: the BRRD (Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive) and, for the banking union, the SRM (Single Resolution Mechanism). Where do covered deposits sit when the worst happens?

5.1 The creditor hierarchy — covered deposits are super-preferred

In insolvency or resolution, claims are paid in a strict order (the Haftungskaskade). Covered deposits enjoy a super-preference: they rank above other ordinary unsecured claims, and the DGS — having paid the depositors — steps into that super-preferred rank by subrogation. Schematically (highest priority first):

Rank (high → low)Claim
1Secured / collateralised claims, certain preferred claims
2Covered deposits (and the DGS by subrogation) — super-preferred
3Eligible deposits of natural persons & SMEs above €100k — preferred
4Other ordinary unsecured claims (incl. large-corporate deposits)
5Subordinated debt → AT1/T2 → CET1 (loss-absorbing capital)

The super-preference is what makes the DGS's recovery from the estate relatively good — but "relatively good and slow" is still worse than "never paid out". Hence prevention.

5.2 How the RTFA + Ampelung feed the decision

The chain the Leitfaden builds is exactly the early-intervention pipeline:

Data (VERA/COREP/FINREP) → RTFA (Problemfall 95% · Extremfall 99,9%) → grün/gelb/rot Ampelung → ÖRS / SRK decision → IPS support before DGS payout

The Sektorrisikokomitee (SRK) is the body that acts on the signals: it consumes the RTFA output and the RED/YELLOW reports and decides whether and how to deploy IPS support. The DGS payout is the branch of the tree the whole apparatus is designed to prune.

5.3 Funding & moral hazard

Two design features fight the moral hazard that any guarantee creates (M1: insured depositors stop monitoring the bank, so the bank can take more risk):

  • Risk-based, ex-ante contributions regulatory — riskier banks pay more up front, internalising some of the risk they impose on the fund, and the fund exists before a crisis rather than being levied during one.
  • The 0,8% target of covered deposits regulatory — a credible pre-funded pot, sized to the insured exposure.
  • IPS prevention on top — mutual monitoring + early support reduces the probability of ever drawing the fund. Every prevented failure is a payout that never happens.

6 · Nuances & traps

⚠ Trap 1 — IPS (prevention) ≠ DGS (payout)

The single most-tested confusion. The R-IPS rests on Art. 113(7) CRR and intervenes before failure to keep a member alive (benefit: 0% intra-IPS risk weight + large-exposure waiver). The DGS rests on the DGSD/ESAEG and pays depositors €100.000 after a Feststellung der Nichtverfügbarkeit or resolution. Same sector, different machines, different legal bases.

⚠ Trap 2 — €100.000 is per depositor PER institution, not per account

Open ten accounts at the same bank and you are still covered to €100.000 in total at that bank (joint accounts and certain categories aside). The "per institution" wording is what lets a depositor with two different banks be covered €100.000 at each. verify current status

⚠ Trap 3 — covered deposits are super-preferred in insolvency

They rank above ordinary unsecured creditors; the DGS inherits that super-preference by subrogation after it pays out. Do not assume the DGS recovers pari passu with general creditors — it sits near the top of the cascade.

⚠ Trap 4 — the target level is 0,8% of COVERED deposits

Not 0,8% of total deposits, not of total liabilities, not of RWAs. The denominator is the protected subset — the exposure the fund actually insures. Getting the denominator wrong overstates or understates the required fund by a lot.

⚠ Trap 5 — the ÖRS in the Leitfaden is the sector Sicherungseinrichtung running the Früherkennung; the current DGS membership must be verified

The Leitfaden's ÖRS is the body operating the R-IPS and the early-warning system. Which statutory deposit-guarantee scheme the sector's banks belong to today (single AUSTRIA scheme vs a sector arrangement) is a separate question — check the FMA register, do not infer it from the IPS role or from memory.

Recap

  • The deposit guarantee exists to break bank-run coordination failures (M1) and underpin confidence; it is the post-failure backstop, distinct from capital (loss absorption) and the IPS (prevention).
  • DGSD (2014/49/EU): €100.000 per depositor per institution; payout toward 7 working days; target 0,8% of covered deposits from risk-based ex-ante contributions; temporary high balances get extra cover. verify current deadlines/targets
  • ESAEG transposes it in Austria (covered deposits, contributions, Anlegerentschädigung €20.000); since 2019 a single scheme Einlagensicherung AUSTRIA for most banks — the current Raiffeisen arrangement must be verified on the FMA register.
  • IPS (Art. 113(7) CRR) = prevention before failure (0% intra-IPS RW + large-exposure waiver); DGS = compensation after failure (€100k). An IPS-run DGS may use its funds for alternative (prevention) measures if cheaper than payout — and the Früherkennung's Ampelung is the trigger that makes early action possible.
  • Covered deposits are super-preferred in the creditor hierarchy; the SRK acts on the RTFA/Ampelung signals to deploy IPS support before the DGS is ever needed.
