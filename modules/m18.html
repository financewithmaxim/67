Module 18 — IRB parameter estimation: PD, downturn LGD, CCF & the Margin of Conservatism

Under the internal-ratings-based approach a bank stops accepting the regulator's risk weights and starts manufacturing them — from three estimated parameters: the probability of default (PD), the loss given default (LGD) and the exposure at default (EAD), the latter driven by a conversion factor (CCF) on the undrawn portion of a commitment. This module derives each parameter from its raw data — a default flag, a stream of discounted recoveries, an undrawn line — and then bolts on the two things examiners care about most: the appropriate adjustment for an unrepresentative data period and the Margin of Conservatism (MoC), the structured cushion that turns a fragile best estimate into a defensible regulatory number. We close by locating the Raiffeisen primary bank in this picture: mostly Standardansatz or Foundation-IRB, where LGD is handed to you as a fixed 45% — which is precisely why the Leitfaden can quietly reuse that constant and spend its modelling energy on the PD.

By the end of this module you can…

  • Split a rating system into its two jobs — rank-ordering (Diskriminierung) and calibration (Kalibrierung) — and explain why a system can be excellent at one and useless at the other
  • Calibrate a PD curve to a long-run central tendency, apply the correct CRR input floor (Art. 163 for retail, Art. 160 for corporate), and articulate the TTC-vs-PIT trade-off the Leitfaden's Früherkennung lives inside
  • Compute a default-weighted realised LGD from a workout: discount recoveries to the default date with the prescribed risk-free + 5% rate, net direct and indirect costs, fold in the cure rate, and explain why downturn LGD exceeds the long-run average
  • Estimate a CCF for the undrawn portion of a commitment and turn it into an EAD, without conflating a drawn balance with a 100% conversion factor
  • Distinguish the long-run-average 'appropriate adjustment' (CRR Art. 181(1)(b)) for unrepresentative data periods from the Margin of Conservatism, and decompose the MoC into categories A, B and C per EBA/GL/2017/16 without double-counting
  • Write down the prescribed maturity adjustment b(PD) and the asset-correlation formulas (corporate, SME, residential mortgage, QRRE) and explain why a retail primary needs PDs but inherits a regulatory LGD

1 · Motivation — why this exists

In the Standardansatz (standardised approach, Module 6) the risk weight is a lookup: a table maps an external rating, or a regulatory exposure class, to a fixed percentage. The bank contributes almost no information about its own borrowers. The internal-ratings-based approach — auf internen Ratings basierender Ansatz, IRB — replaces that lookup with a promise: we know our portfolio better than a generic table does, and we will quantify that knowledge as parameters that feed the supervisory formula.

The supervisory formula (the Vasicek single-risk-factor model, derived in Module 14) takes the parameters and returns a capital requirement \(K\):

$$ K = \text{LGD}\cdot\left[\,N\!\left(\frac{N^{-1}(\text{PD})+\sqrt{\rho}\,N^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right)-\text{PD}\,\right]\cdot \frac{1+(M-2.5)\,b}{1-1.5\,b} $$
where \(\text{PD}\) is the one-year probability of default, \(\text{LGD}\) is loss given default, \(\rho\) the asset correlation, \(M\) the effective maturity (Restlaufzeit), \(b\) the maturity adjustment and \(N(\cdot)\) the standard normal CDF. RWA \(= 12.5\cdot K\cdot \text{EAD}\). The maturity term is dropped entirely for the retail exposure classes — there is no \(M\), no \(b\) — which is why a retail primary can largely ignore it.

Everything in IRB reduces to estimating the quantities the formula eats — PD, LGD, the EAD that scales \(K\), and (outside retail) \(M\) — and then defending those estimates to a supervisor who assumes you have been optimistic. The defence is structured: the appropriate adjustment for an unrepresentative history and the Margin of Conservatism (Sicherheitsmarge), the two ideas that tie this module together.

▶ The one idea

IRB is not build a model. It is estimate a handful of numbers from messy historical data, prove they are representative of what you will lend tomorrow, and add a disciplined amount of conservatism — in the right direction — wherever the data is weak. The model is the easy part; the estimation discipline is the exam.

2 · Concept & logic

2.1 A rating system does two separate jobs

A rating system (Ratingsystem) must be judged on two independent axes that beginners constantly conflate:

PropertyGermanQuestion it answersTypical metric
Rank-ordering / discriminationTrennschärfe / DiskriminierungDo worse grades default more often than better grades? Is the ordering right?AUC / Gini (AR), Kolmogorov–Smirnov
CalibrationKalibrierungDoes grade B's assigned PD of, say, 1.2% actually match its realised default rate? Is the level right?Binomial / Hosmer–Lemeshow test, Brier score

These are orthogonal. A model can have a near-perfect Gini (it always ranks the eventual defaulter below the survivor) yet be catastrophically mis-calibrated (it assigns every grade a PD ten times too low). Conversely a coarse model with mediocre discrimination can be beautifully calibrated on average. IRB validation (Module 19) tests both; the IRB estimation we do here is mostly about the second — getting the level right — because capital scales with the level, not with the Gini.

▶ Why it matters

The Früherkennung (early-warning) logic of the ÖRS Leitfaden is fundamentally a rank-ordering instrument: it sorts exposures into watch / sub-standard buckets so attention flows to the right files. It is not, by itself, a calibrated PD model. Keep the two mentally separate or you will defend an early-warning trigger as if it were a regulatory PD, and the Aufsichtsrat will catch you.

2.2 Through-the-cycle versus point-in-time

A PD can be measured two ways. A point-in-time (PIT) PD answers what is this borrower's default probability over the next 12 months given today's macro conditions? — it rises in recessions, falls in booms. A through-the-cycle (TTC) PD answers what is this borrower's default probability averaged over a representative economic cycle? — it is deliberately insensitive to the current state of the world.

Basel/CRR capital wants a stable, TTC-leaning PD so that risk-weighted assets do not whipsaw with the cycle and force pro-cyclical capital raises in the worst possible moment. IFRS 9 expected-credit-loss accounting (Module 21) wants the opposite — a PIT PD that reacts immediately. The Leitfaden's early-warning signals are PIT by nature (they fire on fresh deterioration). The trade-off below is the single most examinable tension in the whole subject.

▶ Regulatory anchor

The CRR requires PD estimates to reflect a long-run average of one-year default rates per grade (CRR Art. 180), which mechanically pushes the estimate toward TTC. The EBA's IRB repair programme (EBA/GL/2017/16, PD & LGD estimation) operationalises this as calibration to a central tendency over a historical observation period of at least five years for retail and a longer minimum where data are available; the exact minima and any transitional windows are live values to confirm.

3 · Derivation

3.1 PD: from default flags to a calibrated grade PD

Start with the raw object: a binary default flag. Borrower \(i\) in year \(t\) either defaults (\(d_{i,t}=1\)) within the 12-month outcome window or does not (\(d_{i,t}=0\)). Default is defined per CRR Art. 178 — 90 days past due or unlikely-to-pay (UTP), the definition you will have implemented in your data per Module 12.

The observed default rate in grade \(g\) and year \(t\) is the simple count ratio:

$$ \widehat{DR}_{g,t} = \frac{\#\{i\in g \text{ at start of } t : d_{i,t}=1\}}{N_{g,t}} $$
where \(N_{g,t}\) is the number of non-defaulted obligors in grade \(g\) at the start of year \(t\). Obligors that default are counted in the numerator; the denominator is the cohort at risk.

The long-run average default rate (langfristige Ausfallrate) for grade \(g\) is then a weighted average across the \(T\) years of the observation window. The choice of weighting is itself a modelling decision:

$$ \overline{DR}_g^{\text{(obligor-wt)}} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \#\text{defaults}_{g,t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} N_{g,t}} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \overline{DR}_g^{\text{(year-wt)}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{DR}_{g,t} $$
where the left form weights each obligor-year equally (large cohorts dominate); the right form weights each year equally (a quiet year and a crisis year count the same). The year-weighted (arithmetic) mean is generally the more conservative and the more central-tendency faithful, because it does not let a few benign high-volume years dilute the cycle.

Calibration to a central tendency. The grade-level long-run averages above give a calibration target per grade. But a scorecard typically produces a continuous score that we must map to PDs. We pick a calibration function (often a logistic or a scaling of a master-scale) and choose its parameter(s) so that the portfolio-weighted average PD equals the portfolio central tendency \(\overline{CT}\):

$$ \sum_{i} w_i\,\widehat{PD}_i \;\stackrel{!}{=}\; \overline{CT}, \qquad \overline{CT} = \text{long-run average default rate of the whole calibration segment.} $$
where \(w_i\) are exposure or obligor weights. A common one-parameter device is a multiplicative scaling \(\widehat{PD}_i = \min(1,\;\kappa\cdot p_i^{\text{raw}})\) with \(\kappa\) solved so the constraint holds. This preserves rank-ordering (it is monotone) while fixing the level — the clean separation of Section 2.1 in action.
▶ The appropriate adjustment (distinct from MoC)

If the historical observation period does not contain a representative mix of good and bad years — a young portfolio observed only through a benign decade, say — CRR Art. 181(1)(b) (for LGD) and the parallel PD long-run-average requirement oblige you to add an appropriate adjustment so the estimate reflects a full cycle. This is not the Margin of Conservatism. The appropriate adjustment corrects a known structural gap in the long-run average (a missing bad year); the MoC adds a cushion for residual uncertainty on top of an already best-estimated parameter. A defender must keep these two separate: an inspector who finds you have folded the missing-downturn correction into Category-C MoC, or vice versa, will mark it as a methodological error — and as a double-count if both are present.

▶ ÖRS modelling decision

Where do the PDs come from for a primary bank? Not, usually, from the primary itself — a single Raiffeisenbank has neither the default count nor the staff to calibrate a grade-level PD curve. They come from the pooled sector rating operated centrally (Sektorrating), which aggregates defaults across many primaries to obtain statistically stable long-run averages. This is a deliberate ÖRS modelling decision exploiting proportionality and pooling: the trade-off is representativeness — the pooled PD must genuinely fit each primary's local book, which is exactly what the representativeness analysis (Section 6) must prove. The alternative — each primary self-calibrating — fails on data volume.

3.2 The PD input floor — and which article applies

However good the data, the CRR refuses to let any PD fall below a hard floor, to prevent a near-zero PD from collapsing capital toward zero on supposedly riskless exposures. Crucially, the floor lives in different articles for different exposure classes, and getting the citation right is part of defending the number.

▶ Regulatory anchor — read the article carefully

The PD input floor of 0.03% is set by CRR Art. 163(1) for retail exposures and by CRR Art. 160(1) for corporate/institution exposures. Because the primaries' Sektorrating PDs are retail-pooled, it is Art. 163 that floors a retail-grade PD — citing Art. 160 for a retail floor is a real error. Note also that CRR III (applicable from 2025) revisited several IRB input floors; treat the 0.03% value as a live figure under the current regime rather than a settled constant for an institution operating in 2026. The PD floor is a regulatory value: you do not get to argue your best grades are safer than the floor for capital purposes, even if your data says so. The trade-off is risk-sensitivity surrendered for a hard backstop against optimistic tails.

3.3 LGD: from a workout to a default-weighted realised loss rate

LGD is \(1\) minus the recovery rate, measured in present-value terms at the moment of default. Consider a defaulted facility with exposure at default \(EAD\). During the workout (Abwicklung) the bank receives a stream of recoveries \(R_k\) at times \(t_k\) (collateral sale proceeds, partial repayments) and incurs workout costs \(C_k\) (legal fees, servicing — both direct and an allocation of indirect costs). The realised LGD on a single facility is:

$$ \text{LGD}^{\text{realised}}_j = 1 - \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k} \frac{R_{j,k} - C_{j,k}}{(1+r)^{\,t_k}}}{EAD_j} $$
where \(r\) is the discount rate used to bring each cash flow back to the default date (not to today), \(t_k\) is the time from default to cash flow \(k\) in years, \(R_{j,k}\) are recoveries and \(C_{j,k}\) the workout costs at \(t_k\). Recoveries received years after default are worth less — discounting is not optional, it is the whole point.
▶ Two prescriptions you must get right

(i) The discount rate. EBA/GL/2017/16 does not let you use the facility's effective interest rate (Effektivzinssatz). It prescribes a risk-free rate plus a fixed add-on of 5% (a spread reflecting the risk premium of an uncertain recovery stream). Defending an EIR-based discount rate to an inspector is a losing argument — the EBA deliberately moved away from EIR discounting. (ii) The averaging basis. CRR Art. 181(1)(a) requires the regulatory LGD to be a default-weighted average of realised LGDs — each default event counts once — not an exposure-weighted or facility-count-weighted average. Large facilities do not get extra votes in the long-run LGD.

Three refinements the EBA guidelines make mandatory:

  • Cure rate (Gesundungsrate) and repeated defaults. Some defaults cure — the obligor returns to performing without the bank realising a loss. But a cure only counts if the obligor stays cured beyond the probation / cure period (a maximum of nine months under the EBA default-definition framework, Module 12); an obligor who re-defaults inside that window is treated as the same continuing default, not a fresh one. When the same obligor defaults multiple times across the history, you must decide consistently whether to treat them as one or several default events when building both the LGD sample and the cure-rate sample — otherwise you double-count recoveries or cures. The pool LGD is a mixture: \(\text{LGD} = (1-\pi_{\text{cure}})\cdot \text{LGD}_{\text{loss}} + \pi_{\text{cure}}\cdot \text{LGD}_{\text{cure}}\), where \(\text{LGD}_{\text{cure}}\) is small but not necessarily zero (lost interest, costs).
  • Incomplete workouts. Facilities still in workout at the data cut-off have only partial recoveries observed. Ignoring them biases LGD — usually downward if early collateral recovery is banked but the slow drip of later loss is unseen. They must be handled with a methodology, not dropped.

3.4 Why downturn LGD exceeds the long-run average

The Vasicek formula already stresses the PD to the 99.9th percentile of the systematic factor. It does not stress the LGD — \(\text{LGD}\) enters as a multiplicative constant. But empirically, LGD and PD are positively correlated through the cycle: in a downturn, more borrowers default and collateral values fall, so recoveries shrink exactly when defaults spike. A long-run-average LGD plugged into a downturn-PD formula therefore understates capital, because it assumes recoveries are as good in the crash as on average.

▶ Why it matters

This is the conceptual heart of downturn LGD (Abschwung-LGD, EBA/GL/2019/03). The fix is to feed the formula an LGD calibrated to economic-downturn conditions — \(\text{LGD}_{\text{downturn}} \ge \overline{\text{LGD}}\) — so that the LGD term reflects the same bad state of the world the 99.9% PD already assumes. If no material downturn effect can be evidenced, the guideline prescribes an add-on as a fallback (a minimum margin over the long-run LGD).

3.5 CCF / EAD — the conversion applies to the UNDRAWN portion

For a fully drawn term loan there is nothing to convert: the drawn balance is the EAD by definition. The credit conversion factor only enters when there is an undrawn commitment (offene Zusage) — an overdraft line, a revolving credit — because the obligor will typically draw more as it slides toward default. The CCF (Kreditumrechnungsfaktor) estimates how much of the currently undrawn limit converts to drawn exposure by default:

$$ EAD = E_{\text{drawn}} + \text{CCF}\cdot\underbrace{\bigl(L - E_{\text{drawn}}\bigr)}_{\text{undrawn portion}} $$
where \(E_{\text{drawn}}\) is the currently drawn balance, \(L\) the committed limit, and \(L-E_{\text{drawn}}\) the undrawn portion. \(\text{CCF}\in[0,1]\) (CRR III now also imposes CCF floors). The CCF is estimated from observed reference-date-to-default migrations of the undrawn balance.
⚠ Trap — do not say the drawn balance "takes a CCF of 100%"

A drawn, on-balance-sheet exposure does not take a 100% CCF; it is simply already at EAD because it is already drawn. The CCF is a property of the off-balance-sheet / undrawn commitment only. For retail, EAD is estimated at the pool level alongside PD and LGD, and the estimated CCF is applied to the undrawn portion of off-balance-sheet items; Foundation-IRB and the Standardansatz instead use prescribed regulatory CCFs for those off-balance items. Conflating the drawn balance with a conversion factor is a conceptual error an inspector will flag immediately.

3.6 Maturity, the b-adjustment and asset correlation — the rest of the formula

PD, LGD and EAD are the estimated inputs, but the formula also needs the maturity term and \(\rho\), which are prescribed functions, not free estimates. For the non-retail classes the maturity adjustment is:

$$ b(\text{PD}) = \bigl(0.11852 - 0.05478\cdot\ln(\text{PD})\bigr)^2, \qquad M^{\text{F-IRB}}=\text{2.5 (regulatory default)} $$
where \(b\) smooths the capital charge for longer maturities; under Foundation-IRB \(M\) is fixed at 2.5 years (with limited exceptions), so a primary on F-IRB does not estimate \(M\). For the retail classes there is no maturity adjustment at all — the term \(\tfrac{1+(M-2.5)b}{1-1.5b}\) is dropped.

The asset correlation \(\rho\) is likewise prescribed. The values you must be able to quote to defend a retail primary's capital number are:

Exposure classPrescribed correlation \(\rho\)
Corporate / institution / sovereign\( \rho = 0.12\cdot\frac{1-e^{-50\,\text{PD}}}{1-e^{-50}} + 0.24\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac{1-e^{-50\,\text{PD}}}{1-e^{-50}}\Bigr) \)
SME firm-size adjustmentsubtract \(0.04\cdot\bigl(1-\frac{S-5}{45}\bigr)\) from the corporate \(\rho\) (turnover \(S\) in €m, capped 5–50)

For a residential-mortgage-heavy primary the fixed 0.15 correlation, not a PD-dependent curve, is what drives the capital number — which is why senior management's intuition that safer mortgages should obviously cost little capital is only partly right.

3.7 The Margin of Conservatism

Every estimate above is uncertain. EBA/GL/2017/16 forbids you to present a single best guess; it requires the final regulatory parameter to be the best estimate plus a quantified, categorised Margin of Conservatism:

$$ \theta^{\text{final}} = \theta^{\text{best}} + \text{MoC}, \qquad \text{MoC} = \text{MoC}_A + \text{MoC}_B + \text{MoC}_C $$
where \(\theta\) is any parameter (PD, LGD, CCF). The categories are: A — data and methodological deficiencies (missing data, proxies, definitional changes in default, sampling gaps); B — relevant changes to underwriting, collection policy or risk appetite that make the past an imperfect guide to the future; C — general estimation error (the pure statistical uncertainty of the point estimate, e.g. a confidence-interval add-on). The MoC must be applied at the level of the final estimate.
⚠ Trap — Category C must not double-count A and B

The MoC is not a slush fund you tune until capital looks comfortable, and it is not a substitute for fixing the data or for the Art. 181(1)(b) appropriate adjustment. Each component must be traced to a specific identified deficiency (A), a specific change (B) or a specific quantified statistical error (C). The General Estimation Error (Category C) covers only the residual statistical uncertainty of the point estimate; it must not re-capture the data/methodology deficiencies already quantified in A or the change effects already in B. An undocumented, lumped-together MoC that double-counts adjustments is the fastest way to fail a TRIM-style on-site inspection.

4 · Worked example

We run the three estimation tasks the syllabus demands: calibrate a PD to a central tendency, compute a default-weighted, properly discounted realised LGD, and add a MoC.

4.1 Calibrating a retail PD to the long-run central tendency

A retail calibration segment has a scorecard producing raw PDs. Over a 5-year window the year-by-year observed default rates were:

YearCohort \(N_t\)Defaults\(\widehat{DR}_t\)
20192,000180.90%
20202,100522.48%

20231,900170.89%

Obligor-weighted long-run average: total defaults \(=18+52+31+20+17=138\); total obligor-years \(=2000+2100+2050+1950+1900=10{,}000\). So \(\overline{DR}^{\text{obl}}=138/10{,}000=1.38\%\).

Year-weighted (arithmetic) mean: \(\tfrac{1}{5}(0.90+2.48+1.51+1.03+0.89)\% = \tfrac{6.81}{5}\% = 1.362\%\). The two agree closely here because cohort sizes are stable; we adopt the year-weighted figure \(\overline{CT}=1.362\%\) as the central tendency, since it gives the crisis year (2020) equal standing. (Had the window contained no bad year, we would additionally owe an Art. 181-style appropriate adjustment — Section 3.1 — before any MoC.)

Suppose the scorecard's raw exposure-weighted average PD across this segment is \(p^{\text{raw}}=1.10\%\) — it under-predicts. We solve the scaling \(\kappa\):

$$ \kappa = \frac{\overline{CT}}{p^{\text{raw}}} = \frac{1.362\%}{1.10\%} = 1.238 $$

Every grade PD is multiplied by \(1.238\) (then floored). A grade whose raw PD was \(0.02\%\) becomes \(0.0248\%\) — below the retail PD floor of 0.03% set by CRR Art. 163(1) (not Art. 160, which governs corporates), so it is lifted to \(0.03\%\). The calibration fixed the level; the floor caught the tail.

4.2 Discounted, default-weighted realised LGD

Take one defaulted SME facility with \(EAD = €500{,}000\) as a single default event in the default-weighted sample. The workout discount rate is the prescribed risk-free + 5%; with a risk-free rate of 3% this gives \(r=8\%\) p.a. (this is not the facility's effective interest rate — the EBA prescribes risk-free plus the 5% add-on). Cash flows, measured from the default date:

\(t_k\) (yrs)Recovery \(R_k\)Cost \(C_k\)NetDiscount \((1.08)^{-t_k}\)PV of net
0.5120,0005,000115,0000.9623110,659

3.090,00012,00078,0000.793861,919

Total PV of net recoveries \(= 110{,}659 + 171{,}067 + 61{,}919 = €343{,}644\) (to the nearest euro).

$$ \text{LGD}^{\text{realised}} = 1 - \frac{343{,}644}{500{,}000} = 1 - 0.6873 = 0.3127 = 31.27\% $$

Note what discounting cost: undiscounted net recoveries were \(115{,}000+192{,}000+78{,}000=385{,}000\), an apparent LGD of \(1-385{,}000/500{,}000 = 23.0\%\). Discounting the slow recoveries to the default date raised the realised LGD by more than 8 percentage points. An analyst who forgets to discount understates LGD and therefore capital — a classic finding.

Folding in the cure rate. Across the default-weighted pool, suppose 30% of defaults cure (and stay cured beyond the cure period) with \(\text{LGD}_{\text{cure}}=5\%\), and the \(31.27\%\) above is representative of the loss case. The pool LGD is \( (1-0.30)\cdot 31.27\% + 0.30\cdot 5\% = 21.89\% + 1.50\% = 23.39\%\). This is the long-run-average, default-weighted pool LGD, \(\overline{\text{LGD}}=23.39\%\).

4.3 Adding the Margin of Conservatism

Three deficiencies were identified for this LGD, each in its own category so nothing is double-counted:

CategorySourceAdd-on
A — data/methodIndirect costs only crudely allocated; 18% of workouts still open (incomplete-workout bias)+1.5 pp
B — relevant changeCollateral policy tightened in 2022 → past recoveries flatter than future+0.8 pp
C — estimation errorStatistical CI on the mean LGD (small loss-case sample) — residual only, excludes A/B+1.2 pp
$$ \text{LGD}^{\text{final}} = \overline{\text{LGD}} + \text{MoC} = 23.39\% + (1.5+0.8+1.2)\,\text{pp} = 23.39\% + 3.5\,\text{pp} = 26.89\% $$

Then we apply the downturn overlay: if the estimated downturn effect for this segment is, illustratively, a multiplicative factor of 1.25 (or an additive minimum margin — the value here is purely a hypothetical for the worked example, not a regulatory constant), \(\text{LGD}_{\text{downturn}}=\max\) of the downturn-calibrated estimate and any floor. The number that finally enters the Vasicek formula is this downturn-and-MoC-loaded LGD — never the naive 23.0% an undiscounted spreadsheet would have produced.

▶ Result

From a raw 23.0% (undiscounted, no cure, no MoC, no downturn) to a defensible regulatory LGD of about 26.9% before downturn loading — a swing large enough to change the capital outcome materially. Every step was a named, documented, default-weighted adjustment with the prescribed discount rate. That auditability is the deliverable.

5 · In practice (the sector)

5.1 Where the Raiffeisen primary actually sits

Most Raiffeisen primary banks do not run a full Advanced-IRB (A-IRB) stack with self-estimated LGD and CCF. They run either the Standardansatz or, where IRB is used, the Foundation-IRB (Basis-IRB, F-IRB). The distinction is the crux of this section:

CCF/EAD (off-balance)regulatory CCFregulatory CCFown estimate
Mn/aregulatory 2.5 yrsown estimate
▶ Regulatory anchor

In F-IRB the senior unsecured LGD is fixed by the CRR at 45% (and 75% for subordinated), with prescribed treatment of eligible collateral — and these values are themselves subject to CRR III change, so confirm them for the current regime. This is a regulatory value. The trade-off it buys is stark: the bank surrenders any capital benefit from genuinely strong collateral or recovery practices (it cannot prove its LGD is below the prescribed value), but in exchange it escapes the enormous data, validation and downturn-LGD burden of Section 3.3–3.4. For a small primary with too few defaults to estimate LGD credibly, that is a rational bargain — proportionality made concrete. (Note: the prescribed F-IRB LGD applies to the relevant non-retail classes; pure retail exposures under IRB are A-IRB by construction, which is why the sector's retail PD-only posture leans on the Standardansatz and the F-IRB constant rather than self-estimated retail LGD.)

5.2 Why the Leitfaden can use a fixed LGD constant

This is the question senior management will ask, so answer it cleanly. The Leitfaden's Früherkennung and its capital-style calculations operate in a world where the primary's IRB (or quasi-IRB internal) treatment already takes the senior-unsecured LGD as the regulatory 45%. The Leitfaden therefore does not need to estimate LGD at all — it inherits the constant from the underlying capital regime. Its modelling energy goes entirely into the PD dimension: the sector rating and the early-warning overlay that moves an exposure's effective credit-quality bucket. Fixing LGD at the regulatory constant is, from the Leitfaden's standpoint, less an assumption than a consequence of the regime the primaries already inhabit.

▶ ÖRS modelling decision

Reusing the regulatory 45% LGD inside the Leitfaden is a ÖRS modelling decision grounded in proportionality and in consistency with the underlying capital treatment. The trade-off: simplicity and regulatory alignment versus risk-sensitivity. A primary with a heavily secured mortgage book is genuinely over-capitalised at a 45% LGD — but capturing that benefit would require it to become A-IRB, with all the estimation machinery of this module. The sector judges that trade not worth it for most primaries. Cross-reference Module 6 (Standardansatz mechanics) and Module 14 (the Vasicek formula the LGD feeds).

5.3 Where the PD genuinely comes from

So if LGD is a constant and the off-balance CCF is regulatory, the only estimated parameter that drives the primary's capital is the PD — supplied by the central, pooled Sektorrating calibrated as in Section 3.1. The primary's local job is then (a) assign obligors to grades, (b) apply overrides and early-warning signals (the Leitfaden) and (c) trust the centrally calibrated, MoC-loaded, floor-respecting PD. This is why everything in this course about PD calibration matters to a primary, while almost nothing about downturn LGD does — until and unless the institution migrates to A-IRB.

6 · Nuances & traps

6.1 Representativeness

The single load-bearing assumption of pooled estimation: the data used to calibrate must be representative of the portfolio it will be applied to — in obligor mix, default definition, lending standards and economic conditions. EBA/GL/2017/16 makes representativeness a formal, evidenced requirement. A pooled sector PD applied to one primary's idiosyncratically agricultural or tourism-heavy book must be shown to fit, or a Category-A / Category-B MoC must compensate. It's the sector number, it must be fine is not an argument an inspector accepts.

⚠ Trap

Changing the default definition (Module 12) mid-history silently breaks representativeness: defaults counted under the old 90-days-past-due materiality threshold are not comparable to defaults under the new EBA materiality thresholds. Either restate the history or carry an explicit Category-A MoC. Auditors will reconcile your default counts to the definition in force at each date.

6.2 Human judgement and overrides

Rating systems permit overrides (Überschreibungen) — an analyst moving an obligor off the model grade for reasons the model cannot see. Overrides are legitimate but must be (i) capped in frequency, (ii) documented with reasons and (iii) monitored: a high override rate, or a systematic upward bias (analysts always improving grades), signals either a broken model or grade inflation that will under-state PD and capital. The override rate is a standard validation metric in Module 19.

6.3 The MoC and appropriate adjustment are direction-sensitive

A subtle trap: conservatism must be applied in the direction that increases the parameter for capital. For PD and LGD that means upward. But for a parameter like the cure rate, conservatism means assuming fewer cures (a lower cure rate raises LGD), so a positive add-on to the cure rate is anti-conservative. Mechanically adding a positive margin to every input without thinking about which direction is prudent can accidentally reduce capital. Always ask: does this adjustment move the final capital number up? If not, you have the sign wrong. The same direction-discipline applies to the Art. 181(1)(b) appropriate adjustment.

6.4 Floors interact

The retail PD floor (Art. 163), the corporate PD floor (Art. 160), the LGD/CCF input floors (CRR III) and the aggregate output floor at 72.5% of standardised RWA — phased in from 2025 and stepping up to the endpoint over the following years (see Module 17 for the exact schedule and dates) — all bind at different layers. A portfolio can satisfy every parameter floor and still be lifted by the output floor at the aggregate level. Do not assume that respecting the input floors exhausts your floor obligations.

▶ Auf den Punkt gebracht

IRB heißt: PD, LGD und EAD selbst schätzen — sauber kalibriert (CCF nur auf den offenen Rahmen), ausfallgewichtet, mit dem vorgeschriebenen Diskontsatz (risikofrei + 5%), mit der passenden Langfristanpassung (Art. 181(1)(b)) und einer dokumentierten Sicherheitsmarge (MoC). Die meisten Raiffeisen-Primärbanken nutzen jedoch den Standardansatz oder den Basis-IRB, in dem die LGD regulatorisch auf 45% fixiert ist; deshalb konzentriert sich der Leitfaden auf die PD aus dem Sektorrating und die Früherkennung und übernimmt die 45% LGD schlicht aus dem F-IRB-Regime.

Recap

  • A rating system has two independent jobs — rank-ordering (Trennschärfe, measured by Gini/AUC) and calibration (getting the PD level right); capital scales with the level, and the Leitfaden's early-warning is a rank-ordering tool, not a calibrated PD model
  • PD calibration sets the portfolio-average PD equal to the long-run central tendency (a year-weighted average default rate is the prudent choice); the retail PD floor lives in CRR Art. 163(1), the corporate floor in Art. 160 — both around 0.03% but subject to CRR III, so verify. CRR wants TTC; IFRS 9 and Früherkennung are PIT
  • If the data period lacks a representative bad year, CRR Art. 181(1)(b) demands an 'appropriate adjustment' to the long-run average — this is distinct from, and must not be folded into, the Margin of Conservatism
  • Realised LGD is a default-weighted average (Art. 181(1)(a)) of facility LGDs that discount net recoveries to the default date using the prescribed risk-free + 5% rate (not the effective interest rate); cure rates blend down the pool LGD, cures count only past the ~9-month cure period, and downturn LGD exceeds the long-run average because LGD and PD rise together in a crash
  • CCF converts only the UNDRAWN portion of a commitment; a drawn on-balance exposure is at EAD by definition, not 'a 100% CCF'. F-IRB/Standardansatz use regulatory off-balance CCFs, A-IRB estimates its own. The formula also needs prescribed b(PD)=(0.11852−0.05478·ln PD)² and fixed retail correlations (0.15 mortgage, 0.04 QRRE)
  • Final estimate = best estimate + MoC, decomposed into A (data/method), B (relevant changes) and C (residual estimation error only — no double-counting of A/B); Raiffeisen primaries mostly run Standardansatz or F-IRB with LGD fixed near 45%, so PDs come from the pooled Sektorrating and the Leitfaden reuses 45% as a consequence of the regime
🇦🇹 Zusammenfassung (Deutsch)
  • Ein Ratingsystem leistet zwei getrennte Dinge: Trennschärfe (Rangordnung, Gini/AUC) und Kalibrierung (richtiges PD-Niveau). Kapital hängt am Niveau; die Leitfaden-Früherkennung ist ein Trennschärfe-Instrument, kein kalibriertes PD-Modell.
  • Die PD wird auf eine langfristige zentrale Tendenz kalibriert; der Input-Floor von 0,03% steht für Retail in CRR Art. 163(1), für Firmenkunden in Art. 160 — und ist unter CRR III zu prüfen. CRR möchte Through-the-Cycle, IFRS 9 und Früherkennung sind Point-in-Time.
  • Fehlt im Datenfenster ein repräsentatives schlechtes Jahr, verlangt CRR Art. 181(1)(b) eine 'angemessene Anpassung' des Langfristmittels — diese ist von der Sicherheitsmarge (MoC) zu trennen und nicht doppelt zu zählen.
  • Die regulatorische LGD ist ein ausfallgewichteter Durchschnitt (Art. 181(1)(a)); die Netto-Rückflüsse werden mit dem vorgeschriebenen Satz (risikofrei + 5%, nicht Effektivzins) auf den Ausfallzeitpunkt diskontiert. Gesundungen zählen erst nach der ~9-monatigen Gesundungsperiode; die Abschwung-LGD (EBA/GL/2019/03) liegt über dem Langfristmittel.
  • Der CCF wandelt nur den offenen Rahmen um — eine gezogene Bilanzposition ist per Definition EAD, kein '100%-CCF'. Endschätzung = beste Schätzung + MoC (A Daten/Methode, B relevante Änderungen, C reiner Restschätzfehler, ohne A/B doppelt zu zählen). Primärbanken nutzen meist Standardansatz/Basis-IRB mit LGD ~45%; die PD stammt aus dem Sektorrating.
