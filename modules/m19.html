Module 19 — CRR III credit risk: revised SA, revised IRB & the output floor

CRR III transposes the final Basel III reforms — what the market still calls Basel IV — into directly applicable EU law. For credit risk it rebuilds three things at once: a more risk-sensitive Standardised Approach (SA-CR), a clipped Internal Ratings-Based approach (IRB) with input floors and a withdrawn 1.06 scaling factor, and a new aggregate backstop — the Output Floor — that forces a bank's total RWA to be at least 72.5% of what the standardised rulebook would produce. The floor is the keystone: it caps the capital benefit a bank can extract from its own models, and it is the single mechanism that turns the SA-CR rewrite into a binding constraint for IRB users. This module derives all three and computes a floored bank end-to-end so you can defend the number to the Aufsichtsrat (supervisory board).

By the end of this module you can…

  • Explain why CRR III rebuilt the Standardised Approach (greater Risikosensitivität / risk sensitivity, the Sorgfaltspflicht / due-diligence duty) and apply the revised risk weights to real estate, retail, institutions and corporates including the unrated SCRA path and the revised CCFs.
  • Derive how the revised IRB constrains own estimates through input floors (PD, LGD, CCF), the removal of A-IRB for large corporates and institutions, the revised supervisory LGDs, and the removal of the 1.06 scaling factor.
  • State the Output-Floor mechanism precisely — \(RWA \ge 72.5\% \times RWA_{SA}\) — reproduce the phase-in schedule, identify the EU level of application (highest EU consolidation plus national discretion below), and the transitional reliefs.
  • Compute floored RWA twice (IRB and SA) for a worked bank and identify exactly when and by how much the floor binds.
  • Argue why the floor barely touches a pure-SA Raiffeisenbank but bites the IRB Raiffeisenlandesbank, and quantify the sector-aggregate effect.

1 · Motivation — why this exists

The 2017 Basel III finalisation had one overriding political objective: reduce the unwarranted variability of risk-weighted assets (risikogewichtete Aktiva, RWA) across banks holding economically identical portfolios. Supervisory benchmarking exercises had shown that two banks with the same loans could report capital requirements differing by a third or more, almost entirely because of internal-model latitude. That variability corrodes the one number the whole framework rests on — the denominator of the capital ratio. If RWA is gameable, the 8% minimum is meaningless.

CRR III (the regulation amending Regulation (EU) 575/2013) is the EU's transposition. It attacks the variability problem from three sides at once, and you must hold all three in your head simultaneously because they interlock:

▶ The three-front rebuild

(1) Make the Standardised Approach genuinely risk-sensitive, so that the fallback rulebook is itself a credible measure of risk — not a blunt instrument that overstates good exposures and understates bad ones. (2) Constrain the IRB approach with input floors and scope restrictions, so own estimates cannot drift arbitrarily low. (3) Cap the gap between the two with an aggregate Output Floor: total modelled RWA may never fall below 72.5% of total standardised RWA. The first reform makes the third one a fair benchmark; the second reform shrinks the distance the third one has to travel.

For you in Sector Risk Controlling, the practical question is asymmetric. A pure-SA Raiffeisenbank never computes an internal RWA, so the floor — a ratio of modelled to standardised RWA — is structurally non-binding for it (its ratio is exactly 100%). But it still inherits the revised SA-CR risk weights and the revised credit-conversion factors, which change its capital requirement directly. The Raiffeisenlandesbank (RLB) and any IRB user in the sector feel both: the SA rewrite changes their floor benchmark, and the floor itself can override their model output. This module builds the machinery; Module 20 (sector aggregation and consolidation) carries the numbers up to the Institutsbezogenes Sicherungssystem (IPS / institutional protection scheme) level.

2 · Concept & logic

2.1 What "risk-sensitive standardised" means

Under the old SA, a corporate exposure without an external rating took a flat 100% risk weight, a residential mortgage took 35%, and that was largely the end of the conversation. The revised SA-CR introduces gradation: more rating buckets, splitting of real-estate exposures by loan-to-value (Beleihungsauslauf, LTV), a dedicated treatment for unrated counterparties via a standardised credit-risk-assessment, revised credit-conversion factors on off-balance commitments, and punitive weights for subordinated debt and equity. The price of sensitivity is data: banks must now perform due diligence (Sorgfaltspflicht) and cannot mechanically lean on an external rating.

▶ Due-diligence duty — the asymmetry

CRR III requires institutions to take steps to satisfy themselves that an external rating appropriately reflects the credit risk of the position. The mechanic is strictly one-directional: where due diligence reveals risk higher than the bucket implied by the external rating (ECRA) or the standardised assessment (SCRA), the bank must assign a higher risk weight — the supervisory expectation is at least one bucket up. But due diligence can never reduce a risk weight below the value the ECRA/SCRA table assigns. An external rating is a floor on conservatism, never a discount. regulatory value The duty is mandatory; the proportional how (depth of review per exposure class) is where sector guidance fills in.

2.2 Real estate: the default mechanic, loan-splitting and the hard test

The most consequential SA-CR change for a Raiffeisen balance sheet is real estate, because mortgages dominate the asset side. CRR III offers two mechanics, and which one is the default depends on the exposure:

  • Whole-loan approach: a single risk weight applied to the entire exposure, read off an LTV-bucket table. For qualifying residential real estate where repayment does not materially depend on the property's cash flows, the whole-loan (LTV-table) treatment is in fact the CRR III default for many exposures.
  • Loan-splitting (exposure-to-value) approach: the portion of the loan up to 55% of the property value is treated as well-secured and receives a low preferential risk weight; the residual portion above that threshold takes the risk weight of the counterparty (the unsecured retail or corporate weight that would apply absent the property). Loan-splitting is the general/alternative mechanic — calling it the EU's single "preferred default" overstates it; for qualifying residential RE the LTV-table whole-loan treatment is what most exposures actually run on.

Both depend on the property qualifying for preferential treatment, which requires it to pass conditions on completion, valuation independence, and legal enforceability of the security. Critically, the loan can only attract the preferential real-estate weights if repayment does not materially depend on the cash flows generated by the property itself — the so-called income-producing real estate (IPRE) distinction. Loans whose servicing depends on rents from the financed property are pushed into a harsher schedule because their default rates are empirically more cyclical. A separate, even harsher class catches land acquisition, development and construction (ADC) exposures — financing the acquisition of land and the construction phase before stabilised cash flows exist — which carry a punitive default weight of 150% (with a narrower preferential band for qualifying residential ADC).

▶ The "hard test"

The hard test is the empirical gate for using the preferential real-estate risk weights: the security must demonstrably reduce loss. National data must show that losses on such exposures stay within Basel-specified loss thresholds (broadly, that aggregate losses from residential real-estate lending up to a given LTV do not exceed a small fraction of the outstanding amount in any year). If a jurisdiction's loss experience fails the test, the preferential treatment is withdrawn for that market. regulatory value The competent authority (FMA in coordination with the ECB) administers it; banks cannot self-certify around a failed hard test.

2.3 Off-balance commitments: the revised CCFs

A change that is easy to overlook but materially moves a mortgage- and retail-heavy book is the revised credit-conversion factor (Kreditumrechnungsfaktor, CCF) schedule. The CCF translates an off-balance-sheet commitment into an on-balance-equivalent exposure amount before the risk weight is applied.

▶ Unconditionally cancellable commitments are no longer free

Under the old SA, unconditionally cancellable commitments (UCC — undrawn credit lines the bank can cancel at any time without notice) attracted a 0% CCF, so they generated no RWA at all. CRR III raises this to a 10% CCF: even a cancellable line now contributes \(0.10 \times \text{undrawn}\) to the exposure base. Other commitment CCFs are likewise rebuilt (broadly toward 40% for general commitments). regulatory value For the Raiffeisen retail profile — overdraft facilities, undrawn mortgage tranches, revolving consumer lines — this directly inflates \(RWA_{SA}\), which is exactly the floor benchmark. A driver of the floor denominator that is invisible on the drawn balance sheet, and which you must reconcile carefully.

2.4 Retail, institutions and corporates — including unrated (SCRA)

Retail exposures that meet the regulatory retail criteria (granularity, product type, size cap) keep a preferential weight, with CRR III introducing a finer split — notably a transactor sub-class for revolving credit that is repaid in full each cycle, recognising its lower realised risk. Exposures to institutions and corporates retain an external-ratings-based table (ECRA, the External Credit Risk Assessment Approach), but the headline novelty is the path for unrated counterparties.

▶ SCRA for unrated exposures — the correct grades

Because most Austrian SMEs and many institutions are unrated, the Standardised Credit Risk Assessment Approach (SCRA) matters enormously here. For unrated institutions, SCRA sorts the counterparty into three grades — A, B, C — by reference to whether it meets (and exceeds, with buffers) its minimum regulatory requirements. The risk weights are: Grade A = 40% (with a lower 30% for certain short-term exposures), Grade B = 75% (with 20% for qualifying short-term), and Grade C = 150%. For unrated corporates, a flat 100% generally applies, with a reduced 65% for those identified as investment grade under defined criteria (listed securities outstanding, low leverage, stable cash flow). ÖRS modelling decision Operationalising the investment-grade and SCRA-grade tests consistently across hundreds of unrated counterparties is a sector data-quality project, not a regulatory given. Trade-off: tighter evidence standards earn the 65% / Grade-A weight but raise the cost of proof; a conservative blanket 100% / Grade-B saves effort but inflates RWA and, for IRB peers, raises the floor benchmark for everyone.

⚠ The drafting trap on SCRA institution grades

An earlier draft of this material stated Grade A ≈ 40%, Grade B ≈ 75%, Grade C ≈ 150% — but with B and C transposed in the candidate's memory it is easy to invert them, and many summaries quote the wrong figures. Anchor on the structure: Grade A is the strong, buffer-comfortable institution (40%, short-term 30%); Grade B is "meets minimum but tight" (75%, short-term 20%); Grade C is "near or below minimum / material doubt" (150%). The short-term concessions are easy to forget under examination.

2.5 Subordinated debt, equity and the EU support factors

CRR III raises the risk weight on equity holdings to a terminal 250% (and higher — up to 400% for speculative unlisted holdings), with subordinated debt at 150%. This is deliberately punitive: equity is loss-absorbing capital in someone else's structure, so it should consume capital in yours.

▶ Equity is phased in from 100%, not switched on overnight

The higher equity weights do not apply immediately. CRR III provides a transitional phase-in: the equity risk weight ramps up from the old 100% toward the 250% / 400% terminal values over a transitional period of around five years. regulatory value When you brief management, never present 250%/400% as immediately binding — quote the phase-in year. A defender who states the steady-state weight without the ramp will be corrected by the Aufsichtsrat's adviser.

Against this tightening, the EU retains two reliefs Basel did not mandate — the SME-Unterstützungsfaktor (SME supporting factor) and the Infrastruktur-Unterstützungsfaktor (infrastructure supporting factor), multiplicative discounts on the capital requirement for qualifying exposures.

▶ The EU supporting factors

The SME supporting factor multiplies the own-funds requirement for qualifying SME exposures by 0.7619 on the portion up to a per-obligor threshold of €2.5 million, and by a softer factor of 0.85 on the portion above that threshold; the infrastructure factor applies 0.75 to qualifying high-quality infrastructure. regulatory value These are EU-specific deviations from pure Basel, defended on Verhältnismäßigkeit (proportionality) and real-economy grounds. Note the interaction with the floor: because the supporting factors apply in both the SA and IRB computations, they shrink numerator and denominator together and so do not, by themselves, change whether the floor binds.

3 · Derivation

3.1 The revised IRB: input floors and the lost 1.06

Recall the IRB risk-weight function from Module 12. For a non-defaulted corporate exposure, the capital requirement \(K\) per unit of exposure at default (EAD) is

$$ K = \left[ LGD \cdot N\!\left( \frac{N^{-1}(PD) + \sqrt{R}\, N^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1-R}} \right) - PD \cdot LGD \right] \cdot \frac{1 + (M-2.5)\,b}{1 - 1.5\,b} $$
where \(N\) is the standard-normal CDF, \(R\) the asset correlation, \(M\) the maturity, \(b\) the maturity adjustment, \(PD\) the probability of default and \(LGD\) the loss given default. The bracket is the unexpected-loss portion (the 99.9% conditional loss minus the expected loss \(PD\cdot LGD\)).

RWA is then \(RWA = K \cdot 12.5 \cdot EAD\), the 12.5 being \(1/0.08\). Under the old CRR, this product was multiplied by a 1.06 scaling factor, a calibration fudge inherited from the original IRB roll-out. CRR III removes the 1.06. Mechanically:

$$ RWA_{IRB}^{new} = \frac{RWA_{IRB}^{old}}{1.06} \approx 0.9434 \cdot RWA_{IRB}^{old} $$
where \(1/1.06 = 0.9434\), i.e. a reduction of about 5.7% in IRB RWA from this change alone, all else equal. regulatory value This partly offsets the tightening below — the legislators giveth.

The tightening comes from input floors: regulatory minima on the parameters a bank may feed into the function, designed to stop the function being driven to near-zero by optimistic estimates. The key floors are:

ParameterFloor (indicative)Rationale
PD (all exposures)0.05%No obligor is risk-free; caps the benefit of very-low-PD grades.
LGD — unsecured retail30%Floors recovery optimism on unsecured exposures.
LGD — residential mortgage (retail)5%Recognises collateral but not to zero.
CCF (off-balance, A-IRB)SA value × 50% (exposure-specific minimum)Prevents under-estimation of drawn amounts at default.
⚠ Floors bind at the input, not the output

An input floor reshapes the distribution of modelled RWA across your portfolio; it is not the Output Floor. A PD floor of 0.05% raises \(K\) only for obligors whose own-estimate PD fell below it — typically your best names. Do not conflate the two: input floors are micro (per exposure, inside the function); the Output Floor is macro (per bank, on the aggregate). A bank can be entirely unaffected by input floors and still be slammed by the Output Floor, and vice versa.

3.2 Scope restriction and revised supervisory LGDs

The Advanced IRB (A-IRB) lets a bank estimate both PD and LGD/CCF; Foundation IRB (F-IRB) estimates only PD and takes supervisory LGD/CCF values. CRR III removes A-IRB for two classes where loss data is too sparse to estimate LGD credibly:

  • Exposures to large corporates — entities belonging to a group with total annual sales / consolidated revenue (turnover) above €500 million (the basis is annual revenue/turnover, not total assets): F-IRB only.
  • Exposures to institutions and other financial-sector entities: F-IRB only.
  • Exposures to equities: IRB removed entirely — equities move to the SA.

The logic is purely statistical: you cannot estimate an LGD distribution from a handful of large-corporate defaults, so the framework forces the supervisory LGD rather than a flattering own estimate. The supervisory LGDs themselves were recalibrated under CRR III, and the distinction matters:

▶ The senior-unsecured supervisory LGD split

CRR III lowered the F-IRB supervisory LGD for senior unsecured exposures to non-financial corporates to 40%. The old 45% is retained for senior unsecured exposures to institutions and financial-sector entities. So when large corporates are forced onto F-IRB, the relevant supervisory LGD for their senior unsecured book is 40%, not 45% — quoting 45% for a corporate book is a coefficient error. regulatory value The trade-off is the usual one: F-IRB removes the bank's LGD modelling licence but in return gives it a lower (40%) corporate senior-unsecured supervisory LGD than the old regime, partly cushioning the scope loss.

This restriction matters for the RLB's wholesale book; it is largely irrelevant to a retail Raiffeisenbank, which is on SA anyway.

3.3 The Output Floor — derivation and the EU level of application

Define a bank's total risk-weighted exposure amount under its own approaches (a mix of IRB credit risk, internal-model market risk, operational risk, etc.) as \(RWA_{own}\), and the amount it would report if every exposure used the standardised approaches as \(RWA_{SA}\). The Output Floor sets the regulatory RWA to:

$$ RWA_{reg} = \max\!\Big( RWA_{own},\; f \cdot RWA_{SA} \Big) $$
where \(f\) is the floor factor, rising to its terminal value \(f = 72.5\%\). When \(RWA_{own} \ge f\cdot RWA_{SA}\) the floor is slack and the bank reports its own number; otherwise the floor binds and the bank reports \(f\cdot RWA_{SA}\).

Equivalently, define the bank's model density \(\rho = RWA_{own}/RWA_{SA}\). The floor binds iff \(\rho < f\). A pure-SA bank has \(RWA_{own} = RWA_{SA}\), so \(\rho = 1 > 0.725\) — never binding. The whole point of the SA-CR rewrite (Section 2) is to make \(RWA_{SA}\) a fair yardstick, so that \(f\cdot RWA_{SA}\) is a defensible minimum rather than a penalty.

▶ Where the floor is applied in the EU — a load-bearing detail

The floor is computed on total RWA (credit, market and operational risk together), exposure-by-exposure aggregation, not portfolio cherry-picking. But the EU made a specific choice on the level: the Output Floor applies primarily at the highest level of consolidation in the EU (the EU parent / consolidating entity). CRR III then grants Member-State discretion to also apply the floor at sub-consolidated or individual (solo) level for entities in that jurisdiction. So "solo and consolidated" is only half the story: the binding computation is the highest-EU-consolidation floor, with national discretion layering it below. regulatory value This is exactly the lever the IPS scoping argument (§5.2) turns on — whether a sub-group is floored at all below the top of the house is a national-discretion question, not automatic.

3.4 The phase-in and the transitional reliefs

The floor is introduced gradually so banks can build capital. The factor \(f\) rises over a multi-year ramp:

YearFloor factor \(f\)
202550.0%
202655.0%
202760.0%
202865.0%
202970.0%
2030 onward72.5%

Alongside the ramp, CRR III grants EU-specific transitional reliefs that lower \(RWA_{SA}\) for the floor calculation only (not for actual SA reporting), softening the bite during the transition:

  • Residential real estate: a temporary preferential SA risk weight (e.g. 10% on the well-secured portion under loan-splitting) for low-loss-experience exposures, applied in the floor's \(RWA_{SA}\) until 2032.
  • Unrated corporates: a transitional reduced SA weight of 65% for corporates assessed to have a probability of default not higher than 0.5% (the explicit PD gate, used as an investment-grade proxy), applied in the floor's \(RWA_{SA}\) until 2032, acknowledging the scarcity of external ratings in the EU.
▶ Reliefs flatter the denominator of the floor

Because these reliefs reduce \(RWA_{SA}\), they reduce \(f\cdot RWA_{SA}\) and so make the floor less likely to bind during the transition. They are an EU political choice to avoid a cliff-edge for mortgage-heavy and SME-heavy banks — exactly the Austrian profile. regulatory value The reliefs are time-limited; senior management must plan capital for the un-relieved 72.5% steady state, not the transitional comfort.

4 · Worked example — flooring an IRB bank

Take the (stylised) "Raiffeisenlandesbank Beispiel AG", an IRB user, in reporting year 2030 so \(f = \) 72.5%. We treat total RWA as credit-risk-only for this exercise (set market and operational RWA aside) so the arithmetic isolates the floor mechanic. We compute RWA twice.

4.1 Step 1 — IRB RWA (own estimates)

The bank has three credit-risk sub-portfolios. For each we already have \(K\) from the IRB function (Section 3.1, with input floors and without the old 1.06):

PortfolioEAD (€m)IRB \(K\)\(RWA_{IRB}=K\cdot12.5\cdot EAD\) (€m)
Residential mortgages10,0000.0243,000
SME corporates6,0000.0604,500
Large corporates (F-IRB)4,0000.0643,200
Total20,000—10,700

Check one row: mortgages, \(K=0.024\), so \(RWA = 0.024 \times 12.5 \times 10{,}000 = 3{,}000\). The implied risk-weight density on mortgages is \(3{,}000/10{,}000 = 30\%\). So \(RWA_{own} = €10{,}700\text{m}\).

4.2 Step 2 — SA RWA (the same book, standardised)

Now re-run the identical EADs through the revised SA-CR risk weights. Suppose the real-estate mechanic, the revised CCFs on undrawn tranches and the SCRA give:

PortfolioEAD (€m)SA risk weight\(RWA_{SA}\) (€m)
Residential mortgages10,00040%4,000
SME corporates (unrated, 85% SME-eligible)6,000effective ~82%4,920
Large corporates (unrated, 100%)4,000100%4,000
Total20,000—12,920

So \(RWA_{SA} = €12{,}920\text{m}\). The aggregate model density is

$$ \rho = \frac{RWA_{own}}{RWA_{SA}} = \frac{10{,}700}{12{,}920} = 0.8282 = 82.8\% $$
where \(\rho > \) 72.5%, so at full phase-in this particular bank's floor is slack — its model is not aggressive enough to be capped. Good. Now we perturb it.

4.3 Step 3 — a bank whose model IS aggressive

Replace the IRB column with a bank that has invested heavily in low-PD/low-LGD estimates, producing \(RWA_{own} = €8{,}500\text{m}\) on the same \(RWA_{SA} = €12{,}920\text{m}\). Then

$$ \rho = \frac{8{,}500}{12{,}920} = 0.6579 = 65.8\% \; < \; 72.5\% \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{floor BINDS} $$

The floored RWA is

$$ RWA_{reg} = \max(8{,}500,\; 0.725 \times 12{,}920) = \max(8{,}500,\; 9{,}367) = €9{,}367\text{m} $$
where the bank must hold capital against €9,367m even though its models say €8,500m. The floor add-on is \(9{,}367 - 8{,}500 = €867\text{m}\) of extra RWA — pure regulatory overlay on top of the model.

At 8% that add-on is \(0.08 \times 867 = €69.3\text{m}\) of additional own funds the bank must carry purely because the floor binds. Translate to a CET1 view: if the bank targets a 14% CET1 ratio, the floor consumes \(0.14 \times 867 = €121.4\text{m}\) of CET1 capacity that could otherwise back lending.

▶ Reading the phase-in across years

For this aggressive bank (\(RWA_{own}=8{,}500\)), trace when the floor begins to bite as \(f\) ramps. The floor binds once \(f\cdot 12{,}920 > 8{,}500\), i.e. \(f > 0.6579\). So in 2025 (\(f=\)50%: floor \(=6{,}460\)) and 2026–2028 (\(f\) up to 65%: floor \(=8{,}398\)) the floor is still slack; it first binds in 2029 (\(f=\)70%: floor \(=9{,}044\), add-on €544m) and tightens further in 2030 (add-on €867m). The phase-in is not cosmetic — it literally delays the constraint by years for a moderately aggressive bank.

4.4 Why a pure-SA Raiffeisenbank is untouched

For a pure-SA bank, \(RWA_{own} \equiv RWA_{SA}\). Substituting, \(RWA_{reg} = \max(RWA_{SA},\, 0.725\,RWA_{SA}) = RWA_{SA}\). The floor is mathematically inert. The pure-SA Raiffeisenbank's capital changes only because the SA risk weights and CCFs themselves changed (Section 2) — the mortgage book moves onto the revised real-estate mechanic, undrawn lines pick up the new 10% CCF, the unrated SME runs through SCRA with the supporting factor — but never because of the floor. The floor is a constraint on modelling licence, and a bank with no licence cannot have it revoked.

5 · In practice (the sector)

5.1 Who feels it

The Austrian Raiffeisen sector is overwhelmingly SA at the local-bank level. The IRB footprint concentrates in the Raiffeisenlandesbanken and the central institution. So the floor is a wholesale / central-institution problem, not a primary-bank problem. Your Risk Controlling brief is therefore bifurcated:

Primary RaiffeisenbankSA-CRNone (ρ = 100%)Revised SA risk weights, revised CCFs (RE, SME, retail, undrawn lines)
RaiffeisenlandesbankIRB (partial)Binds where ρ < 72.5%Floor + input floors + A-IRB scope loss + revised supervisory LGD

5.2 The sector-aggregate subtlety

▶ Where the floor is applied in an IPS

The Output Floor applies at the highest level of consolidation in the EU, with Member-State discretion to apply it below (§3.3). For an IPS the economically interesting figure is therefore the consolidated floor at the top of the prudential scope: aggregate the IRB \(RWA_{own}\) and the SA \(RWA_{SA}\) across the consolidation scope, then floor. Because the many SA primary banks contribute \(RWA_{own}=RWA_{SA}\), they raise the consolidated \(\rho\) toward 1 — they dilute the aggressive IRB density of the central institution. ÖRS modelling decision Whether a given primary bank sits inside the prudential consolidation (and whether Austria exercises the discretion to floor sub-consolidated levels) changes the floor arithmetic materially. Trade-off: broad consolidation dilutes the floor (good for capital) but pulls more entities into the heavier reporting and governance perimeter (costly), and national-discretion solo flooring can re-impose the constraint on an IRB sub-group the group dilution would otherwise mask. This scoping decision is one you must be able to defend line-by-line to the Aufsichtsrat.

Concretely: if the central institution alone has \(\rho = 65.8\%\) (binding, from 4.3) but the consolidated group adds €30,000m of pure-SA primary-bank RWA where \(RWA_{own}=RWA_{SA}\), the consolidated density becomes

$$ \rho_{grp} = \frac{8{,}500 + 30{,}000}{12{,}920 + 30{,}000} = \frac{38{,}500}{42{,}920} = 0.8970 = 89.7\% $$
where the consolidated \(\rho_{grp} \gg \) 72.5%: the floor that bound at the central institution solo is comfortably slack at the group — provided Austria does not also exercise the discretion to floor the IRB sub-group on a solo/sub-consolidated basis. Consolidation scope, and the national-discretion question, are themselves capital-management levers.

5.3 What to build in R/SQL

Operationally you need a parallel SA-CR engine running over the same exposure table that feeds the IRB engine. The two must consume one golden source of EAD, collateral, LTV, undrawn commitment amount and counterparty classification, or the ratio \(\rho\) becomes uninterpretable. A defensible pipeline computes, per exposure: IRB \(K\) and RWA; SA risk weight and RWA (applying the revised CCF to undrawn lines, and flagging the transitional reliefs separately so you can show floored RWA with and without reliefs); then aggregates and applies the year-appropriate \(f\) at the correct consolidation level. Reconciling the two engines to a single EAD — and reconciling the CCF-converted off-balance amounts — is, in practice, where most of the audit findings land.

6 · Nuances & traps

⚠ The floor is on RWA, but capital buffers sit on top

The Output Floor recomputes RWA. Pillar 2 requirements, the combined buffer (capital conservation, countercyclical, systemic) and any P2G then apply to the floored RWA. A binding floor therefore cascades: it raises the denominator-driven euro amount of every buffer too. EU law contains a mechanism so that member states can address the interaction between the floor and macroprudential buffers, but the default is that buffers stack on floored RWA. Never quote the floor add-on as if 8% were the only multiplier — the effective marginal capital cost is closer to your total CET1 target ratio.

⚠ The floor does not erase the EAD reduction from credit risk mitigation

Some assume the floor "undoes" the benefit of collateral. It does not directly. CRM (collateral, guarantees) reduces both the IRB and the SA RWA. What the floor caps is the extra benefit IRB modelling extracts beyond what the SA already recognises. If your collateral is fully recognised in the SA too, the floor leaves that benefit intact.

⚠ Input floors and the Output Floor can both bind — and they are not additive in an obvious way

Applying a PD floor raises \(RWA_{own}\), which raises \(\rho\) and makes the Output Floor less likely to bind. So the two constraints partly substitute: a bank heavily hit by input floors is, all else equal, further from the Output Floor. When you brief management, present both, but do not double-count the conservatism — the binding constraint is the max, never the sum.

⚠ Transitional relief is a denominator trick, not real capital

The residential-real-estate and unrated-corporate reliefs lower \(RWA_{SA}\) for the floor only. Your actual SA-reported RWA (for a pure-SA bank, or for the SA portions of an IRB bank) does not get the floor relief. Mixing the two denominators is a classic reconciliation error: keep "SA RWA for reporting" and "SA RWA as floor benchmark" in separate columns.

⚠ Quoting steady-state weights without their phase-ins

Two CRR III weights are phased in, and a defender who states the terminal value as immediate will be corrected: the Output Floor factor (50% in 2025 to 72.5% in 2030) and the equity risk weight (ramping from 100% toward 250%/400% over roughly five years). Always pair a terminal weight with its applicable year.

▶ Kurzfassung (recap, German)

CRR III baut das Kreditrisiko dreifach um: ein risikosensitiverer Standardansatz (SA-CR mit Sorgfaltspflicht, revidiertem Immobilienmechanismus, neuen Kreditumrechnungsfaktoren — u. a. 10% CCF für unbedingt kündbare Zusagen — und SCRA für ungeratete Adressen mit Instituts-Risikogewichten Grade A/B/C von 40%/75%/150%), ein eingeschränkter IRB-Ansatz (Input-Floors für PD/LGD/CCF, kein A-IRB mehr für Großunternehmen und Institute, gesenkte Aufsichts-LGD von 40% für vorrangige unbesicherte Unternehmensforderungen, Wegfall des 1,06-Faktors) und der Output Floor: die RWA müssen mindestens 72,5% der Standard-RWA betragen, eingeführt schrittweise von 50% (2025) bis 72,5% (2030), angewendet primär auf der obersten EU-Konsolidierungsebene mit nationalem Ermessen darunter. Für eine reine SA-Raiffeisenbank ist der Floor strukturell unwirksam (ρ = 100%); für die IRB-nutzende Raiffeisenlandesbank kann er binden und einen Kapitalaufschlag erzwingen. Auf IPS-Ebene verwässern die vielen SA-Primärbanken die aggressive IRB-Dichte, sodass der Konsolidierungskreis — und die nationale Ermessensfrage — selbst ein Kapitalsteuerungshebel ist.

Recap

  • CRR III rebuilds credit-risk RWA on three interlocking fronts: a risk-sensitive revised SA-CR, a constrained IRB, and the Output Floor that caps the gap between them.
  • The revised SA-CR adds risk sensitivity (the revised real-estate mechanic with a 55% well-secured tranche and the hard test, ADC at 150%, SCRA for unrated institutions with Grade A/B/C = 40%/75%/150% and unrated corporates at 100%/65%, revised CCFs — notably unconditionally cancellable commitments rising from 0% to 10% — punitive equity and subordinated-debt weights) and a one-directional due-diligence duty (it can raise but never lower the table weight), retaining EU-specific SME and infrastructure supporting factors.
  • The revised IRB imposes input floors on PD/LGD/CCF, removes A-IRB for large corporates (revenue > €500m) and institutions (F-IRB only) and for equities (SA only), lowers the F-IRB senior-unsecured supervisory LGD for corporates to 40% (45% retained for institutions/financials), and removes the 1.06 scaling factor (~5.7% IRB RWA reduction).
  • The Output Floor sets RWA_reg = max(RWA_own, f·RWA_SA) with f ramping 50% (2025) to 72.5% (2030); it applies primarily at the highest EU consolidation level with Member-State discretion below, binds iff model density ρ = RWA_own/RWA_SA falls below f, and has transitional reliefs lowering the floor's SA benchmark for residential RE and for unrated corporates with PD ≤ 0.5%.
  • Equity weights (250%/400%) and the floor factor are both phased in — quote the year, not just the terminal value.
  • A pure-SA Raiffeisenbank has ρ = 100% and is structurally unaffected by the floor; the IRB Raiffeisenlandesbank can be capped, and at IPS-consolidated level the SA primary banks dilute ρ so consolidation scope (and national-discretion solo flooring) becomes a capital lever.
  • Worked: an aggressive IRB bank with RWA_own = €8,500m against RWA_SA = €12,920m has ρ = 65.8% < 72.5%, so floored RWA = €9,367m — an €867m add-on, ~€69m extra own funds at 8%.
🇦🇹 Zusammenfassung (Deutsch)
  • CRR III setzt die finalen Basel-III-Reformen („Basel IV“) unmittelbar in EU-Recht um und reduziert die ungerechtfertigte RWA-Variabilität über drei Hebel gleichzeitig.
  • Der revidierte Standardansatz (SA-CR) wird risikosensitiver: Sorgfaltspflicht (kann das Tabellengewicht nur erhöhen, nie senken), revidierter Immobilienmechanismus mit 55%-Tranche und „hard test“, ADC mit 150%, SCRA für ungeratete Institute (Grade A/B/C = 40%/75%/150%) und Unternehmen (100%/65%), neue Kreditumrechnungsfaktoren (u. a. 10% für unbedingt kündbare Zusagen statt 0%), höhere Risikogewichte für Beteiligungen und Nachrang — plus EU-eigene SME- und Infrastruktur-Unterstützungsfaktoren.
  • Der IRB-Ansatz wird beschnitten: Input-Floors für PD/LGD/CCF, kein A-IRB mehr für Großunternehmen (Umsatz > €500m) und Institute (nur F-IRB), gesenkte Aufsichts-LGD von 40% für vorrangige unbesicherte Unternehmensforderungen (45% bleibt für Institute/Finanzsektor), Beteiligungen in den SA, und Wegfall des 1,06-Skalierungsfaktors.
  • Der Output Floor verlangt RWA_reg = max(RWA_own; f·RWA_SA), wobei f von 50% (2025) auf 72,5% (2030) steigt und primär auf oberster EU-Konsolidierungsebene gilt (nationales Ermessen darunter); er bindet, wenn die Modelldichte ρ < f ist.
  • Für eine reine SA-Raiffeisenbank ist ρ = 100% — der Floor ist strukturell wirkungslos; die IRB-nutzende Raiffeisenlandesbank kann gefloort werden, auf IPS-Konsolidierungsebene verwässern die SA-Primärbanken die Dichte, sofern das nationale Ermessen den Floor nicht auch auf Teilkonzern-/Solo-Ebene anwendet.
