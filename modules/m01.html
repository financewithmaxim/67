Module 1 — Why bank capital regulation exists, and where the ÖRS sits

Before any formula: why do we hold capital against risk at all, how did Basel I→IV and the CRR/CRD build the machinery, and where in that machinery do the Raiffeisen IPS, the ÖRS, and the Früherkennung actually live?

By the end of this module you can…

  • Explain the economic function of bank capital and why a solvent bank can still fail.
  • Sketch the Basel I → II → III → IV evolution and the three-Pillar structure.
  • Map Basel → EU codification (CRR/CRD, the Single Rulebook) and name the supervisors (EBA, ECB/SSM, FMA, OeNB).
  • Locate the R-IPS, the ÖRS, the Sektorrisikokomitee and the deposit-guarantee scheme — and say what the Früherkennung is for.
  • State the legal anchor (§39/§39a BWG) and the three observation views (Solo / KI-Gruppe / IPS).

1 · Motivation — why this exists

A bank is an institution that funds illiquid, risky, long-dated assets (loans) with liquid, safe, short-dated liabilities (deposits). That maturity and risk transformation is the social value of a bank — and also its fragility. Two distinct things can kill it:

  • Insolvency — losses on the asset side exceed the equity cushion, so the bank owes more than it owns. This is what capital protects against.
  • Illiquidity — the bank is solvent on paper but cannot meet payments today because depositors run faster than assets can be turned to cash. This is what liquidity rules (LCR/NSFR, and the Leitfaden Liquidität) protect against.
▶ The core idea capital regulation encodes

Deposits are a public good backed implicitly by the state (deposit guarantee) and the central bank. That creates moral hazard: a bank's owners keep the upside of risky lending but can push the downside onto depositors and the taxpayer. Capital requirements force owners to keep enough skin in the game (Eigenmittel, own funds) that they — not the public — absorb losses first. Capital is the buffer that converts a potential bailout into a private loss absorption.

The whole edifice you operate — the RTFA, the Deckungsmassen, the traffic lights — is one concrete answer to a single question: "Is there enough loss-absorbing substance standing in front of the risk this bank is actually running?" Everything in this course is a way of measuring the two sides of that inequality precisely.

2 · Concept & logic — the regulatory stack

2.1 Expected vs unexpected loss — the conceptual seed of everything

Split the loss on a credit portfolio into two pieces. This split is the single most important idea in the course; we will meet it again in every module.

$$ \text{Loss} \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[L]}_{\text{Expected Loss (EL)}} \;+\; \underbrace{\big(L-\mathbb{E}[L]\big)}_{\text{Unexpected Loss (UL)}} $$
where EL = the average loss you expect "through the cycle" — a cost of doing business, priced into margins and covered by provisions (Wertberichtigungen / Vorsorgen); UL = the deviation of actual loss above that average — the surprise, which is what capital must absorb.
Why this matters for the Früherkennung

The Leitfaden reports credit risk as the unexpected loss (UL) via the IRB formula, and places the expected loss (EL) into the Deckungsmasse as an "excess/shortfall" (EL netted against provisions). Capital covers surprises; provisions cover the average. If you understand why EL and UL are accounted for on opposite sides of the ledger, half of Module 5 and Module 13 is already done.

The conceptual root you must hold onto

Problemfall ≈ Pillar 1 / normative view (95%, "are we above the regulatory minimum after a bad year?"). Extremfall ≈ Pillar 2 / economic view (99.9%, "do we have enough economic substance for our own risk?"). Module 3 builds this out; Module 4 turns it into numbers.

3 · From Basel to your desk — the EU codification

Basel is a standard agreed by the Basel Committee (BCBS) in Basel; it has no legal force. The EU turns it into binding law as a directly applicable Regulation plus a transposed Directive:

$$ \text{Basel standard} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{CRR}}_{\text{Regulation (EU) 575/2013}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{CRD}}_{\text{Directive 2013/36/EU}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{BWG §39/§39a}}_{\text{Austrian transposition}} $$
CRR (Capital Requirements Regulation) — directly applicable in every member state, no national transposition needed. Contains the hard numbers: own funds (Part Two), credit-risk IRB (Art. 153/154), CVA (Title VI), large exposures (Part Four), leverage (Part Seven), the default definition (Art. 178).
CRD (Capital Requirements Directive) — must be transposed into national law; contains buffers, supervision, governance, the ICAAP/SREP mandate.
Single Rulebook — the CRR/CRD plus the EBA's binding technical standards (RTS/ITS) and guidelines that fill in the detail (e.g. the materiality RTS behind Art. 178, the IRRBB SOT under EBA/GL/2018/02).
⚖️ Regulation vs Directive — a distinction examiners love

If the Leitfaden cites a CRR article (e.g. Art. 153), that is directly binding law. If it cites the CRD / BWG (e.g. §39a BWG), that is national law transposing an EU Directive. The Leitfaden itself is neither — it is a sector minimum standard the ÖRS sets for §39a BWG purposes. Keep these three layers straight when you defend a choice: "the CRR forces X; the BWG requires a risk-bearing-capacity process; the ÖRS Leitfaden operationalises it as Y."

4 · Worked example — placing the Raiffeisen sector on the map

Worked example — "who watches a single Raiffeisenbank, and against what?"

Take a small Raiffeisenbank (a Primärbank). Trace the layers that bear on it:

  1. Pillar 1 (CRR, directly): it must hold ≥ 8% total capital / 4.5% CET1 against RWAs, plus the capital conservation buffer. → The Solvabilität side-condition (Leitfaden §4.3).
  2. Pillar 2 (§39a BWG → ICAAP): it must run a Risikotragfähigkeitsanalyse covering all material risks. → The Leitfaden is the sector's minimum-standard ICAAP for this (§39a).
  3. R-IPS (Art. 113(7) CRR): it is a member of the Raiffeisen Institutssicherungssystem — a mutual-support scheme that keeps members solvent and liquid. The IPS gets a 0% intra-group risk weight in exchange for joint monitoring.
  4. ÖRS: the Österreichische Raiffeisen-Sicherungseinrichtung eGen runs the Früherkennung across IPS members and is the deposit-guarantee body. Its Sektorrisikokomitee (SRK) and Vorstand/Aufsichtsrat consume the RTFA output.

Result: the same little bank is looked at through three views — Solo (the legal entity), KI-Gruppe §30 BWG (its prudential consolidation, if any), and IPS (the whole sector aggregated). Module 12 shows why these three can give different traffic lights for one bank.

5 · In practice — what the Leitfaden actually says

The Leitfaden grounds itself in §39 and §39a BWG (comprehensive risk capture, measurement, monitoring and steering), and in the statutory duties of Bankprüfer (auditors) and Einlagensicherungen (deposit-guarantee schemes). It is the sector's standard for quantifying risks and setting them against Risikodeckungsmassen, feeding the ÖRS Aufsichtsrat, the ÖRS Vorstand, and the SRK. It entered into force 1 January 2024 and applies to Zentral- und Sonderinstitute, Raiffeisenbanken on a solo basis, Raiffeisenlandesbanken (also for the KI-Gruppe where applicable), and the R-IPS.

It explicitly invokes the Proportionalitätsgedanke (proportionality principle): the choice and depth of methods scales with the size and complexity of the institution. This is the licence under which the ÖRS replaces hard regulatory machinery with the simplifications you will spend most of this course dissecting.

🟣 Your first ÖRS-choice sighting

The Leitfaden says it captures the §39a BWG risk catalogue but "die weitere Gliederung und Behandlung der Risikoarten folgt nicht der gesetzlichen Gliederung" — the structure of how risks are grouped and treated is an ÖRS modelling decision, not the legal one. Throughout the course, when the Leitfaden's organisation diverges from the CRR's, that is proportionality at work — and you must be able to name the trade-off (comparability & tractability vs. theoretical purity).

6 · Nuances & traps

⚠ Trap 1 — "capital" is not "cash"

Capital (Eigenmittel) is a liability-side concept: it is the equity-like cushion funding the assets, available to absorb losses. It is not a pot of money sitting idle. Liquidity is a separate problem (Module 9). A bank can be well-capitalised and still fail for lack of liquidity — Northern Rock, 2007.

⚠ Trap 2 — the IPS is not the deposit-guarantee scheme

The R-IPS (Art. 113(7) CRR) is a preventive mutual-support mechanism: it intervenes before failure to keep a member alive. The DGS / Einlagensicherung (DGSD/ESAEG) pays out after failure, up to €100,000 per depositor. They are legally distinct, even though in the Raiffeisen sector both sit at/near the ÖRS. Module 17 untangles them.

⚠ Trap 3 — Solo ≠ Group ≠ IPS

"What is the bank's risk utilisation?" has no single answer. The Leitfaden requires the risk and the Deckungsmasse to be measured on a consistent basis — Solo with Solo, KI-Gruppe with KI-Gruppe, IPS with IPS. Mixing a Solo risk with a Group Deckungsmasse is the classic rookie error. Hold this until Module 12.

Recap

  • Banks exist to transform maturity and risk; capital protects against insolvency, liquidity rules against illiquidity.
  • EL is a cost (covered by provisions); UL is a surprise (covered by capital) — they sit on opposite sides of the RTFA ledger.
  • Basel I→IV: from crude buckets → risk-sensitive IRB + three Pillars → buffers/liquidity → output floor & FRTB.
  • Basel → CRR (binding Regulation) + CRD (Directive → BWG §39/§39a) + EBA Single Rulebook; supervised by EBA / ECB-SSM / FMA / OeNB.
  • Problemfall ≈ Pillar 1 (95%); Extremfall ≈ Pillar 2/ICAAP (99.9%).
  • The ÖRS runs the Früherkennung over R-IPS members across three views — Solo / KI-Gruppe §30 BWG / IPS; the DGS is a separate, post-failure payout scheme.
🇦🇹 Zusammenfassung (Deutsch)
  • Eigenkapital schützt vor Insolvenz, Liquiditätsregeln vor Illiquidität — zwei verschiedene Gefahren.
  • Erwarteter Verlust ist über Wertberichtigungen/Vorsorgen abgedeckt; unerwarteter Verlust über das Kapital. Genau so trennt der Leitfaden EL (Excess/Shortfall in der Deckungsmasse) und UL (Risiko).
  • Basel I→IV wird in der EU über die CRR (unmittelbar geltende Verordnung) und die CRD (Richtlinie → BWG §39/§39a) umgesetzt; ergänzt um das EBA-Single Rulebook.
  • Problemfall ≈ Säule 1 (95%), Extremfall ≈ Säule 2 / ICAAP (99,9%).
  • Die ÖRS führt die Früherkennung für die R-IPS-Mitglieder durch — in den Sichten Solo / KI-Gruppe §30 BWG / IPS. Die Einlagensicherung ist davon getrennt.
Source: ÖRS Leitfaden Früherkennung 09/2024, Ch. 1–2; CRR (EU) 575/2013; CRD 2013/36/EU; BWG §39/§39a. Basel IV/CRR III specifics post-date the guide — see Module 16.
