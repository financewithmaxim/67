Module 22 — Stress testing & reverse stress testing

Stress testing is the discipline of asking, in writing and with numbers, "what would it take to break us — and would we survive what is merely plausible?" This module derives the mechanics from first principles: the satellite-model chain from a macro shock to a PD-Migration (rating downgrade) and an LGD-Aufschlag (loss-given-default uplift), how that loss actually reaches CET1 through the IFRS-9-Wertberichtigung (ECL provisioning) and the regulatory EL-Fehlbetrag (EL shortfall) rather than as a raw charge, the incremental credit-risk capital that falls out, and the post-stress CET1 ratio you must defend in the ICAAP's Extremfall (extreme case) against the buffer stack and the MDA (maximum distributable amount) trip-wire. It then inverts the question — Reverse Stresstest (reverse stress test) — to find the scenario that drives the Raiffeisen bank to non-viability, and shows how all of this anchors the Leitfaden's stress approach and the Deckungsmasse (cover pool).

By the end of this module you can…

  • Distinguish Sensitivitätsanalyse (single-factor), Szenarioanalyse (multi-factor, multi-year) and reverse stress testing, and say which question each answers.
  • Derive the satellite-model chain that maps a macro adverse scenario into a PD downgrade and an LGD uplift, and explain why the mapping must be calibrated, not asserted.
  • Trace how loss actually reaches CET1 — through IFRS 9 ECL stage migration in the numerator and the regulatory EL-shortfall deduction for IRB — rather than treating EL as a direct charge.
  • Compute an incremental credit-risk capital number from a stressed PD/LGD and read the resulting post-stress CET1 ratio against the correct hurdle stack (P1 + P2R + combined buffer + P2G) and the MDA trigger.
  • Explain the EU-wide EBA stress test mechanics — static balance sheet, adverse scenario, CET1 trajectory, qualitative quality assurance — and why its output feeds SREP/P2G with no mechanical pass/fail, unlike an institution's own ICAAP stress test.
  • Set up a reverse stress test, state its governance purpose, and connect both the forward and reverse approaches to the Leitfaden's Extremfall and the Deckungsmasse.

1 · Motivation — why this exists

The Säule 1 (Pillar 1) capital machinery — risk-weighted assets, the IRB risk-weight formula, the 8% minimum — is a point-in-time photograph taken under today's conditions. It answers "how much capital do I need against the risk I can see now?" It says nothing about the question that actually keeps a supervisor and an Aufsichtsrat (supervisory board) awake: "what happens to that number when the world turns ugly, and do I still have a bank afterwards?" Stress testing is the forward-looking complement that closes this gap. It is the engine room of the ICAAP's Risikotragfähigkeit (risk-bearing capacity, RTFA) under its Extremfall view — see Module 19 for the normative-vs-economic perspectives and the going-/gone-concern logic this module now stresses.

▶ The core idea

A stress test takes a coherent set of adverse but plausible conditions, pushes them through your portfolio via transmission models, and reports the impact on losses, RWA and ultimately your capital ratio. Its purpose is not prediction — it is to reveal fragility before reality does, so management can act while action is still cheap.

Three regulatory currents converge here. First, the regulatory value CRD requirement (Art. 73 on the internal capital adequacy assessment and Art. 100 on supervisory stress testing, transposed in Austria via the BWG) obliges institutions to run their own internal stress tests as part of sound internal capital adequacy. Second, the EBA/GL/2018/04 Guidelines on institutions' stress testing set the methodological bar for governance, scenario design and reverse stress testing. Third, the ECB's supervisory expectations (the ICAAP-Leitfaden) treat stress testing as a load-bearing pillar of the capital-adequacy story. The ÖRS Leitfaden Früherkennung (early-warning guide) sits underneath all of this: it operationalises the sector-level stress approach for the Austrian Raiffeisen Verbund (network) under the proportionality the smaller institutions are entitled to.

2 · Concept & logic

2.1 Three instruments, three questions

The discipline contains three distinct tools that are frequently — and wrongly — used interchangeably. The distinction is exam-critical.

InstrumentGerman termQuestion answeredInputsDirection
Sensitivity analysisSensitivitätsanalyse"How much does my capital move if one factor shocks?"Single factor (e.g. +200bp rates)Forward, factor → impact
Scenario analysisSzenarioanalyse"What happens under a coherent, multi-year adverse world?"Many factors jointly, multi-period pathForward, scenario → impact
Reverse stress testReverse Stresstest"Which scenario would render me non-viable?"Fixed endpoint (failure); scenario is the unknownBackward, impact → scenario
▶ Why it matters

Sensitivity analysis isolates and ranks vulnerabilities (which lever hurts most?). Scenario analysis tests coherence and second-round effects (do shocks compound?). Reverse stress testing escapes the anchoring bias of "we only test what we can imagine surviving" by starting from death and reasoning back to cause. A risk function that runs only the first two is testing its own optimism.

2.2 Baseline vs adverse

A scenario is defined relative to a Basisszenario (baseline) — the expected macro path — and an adverses Szenario (adverse) — a severe-but-plausible deviation. The capital impact that matters is the incremental deterioration from baseline to adverse, because the baseline path is already embedded in the bank's plan. Severity is calibrated to a target stress: historically the EBA adverse scenarios have been engineered to a cumulative EU GDP deviation from baseline of roughly several percentage points over three years, with deeper national paths where vulnerabilities concentrate.

3 · Derivation — from macro shock to capital

The whole apparatus is a chain of conditional models. Write the macro scenario as a vector path \( \mathbf{x}_t \) (GDP growth, unemployment \(u_t\), residential and commercial property prices, the short and long rate, credit spreads). The job is to propagate \( \mathbf{x}_t \) into the three risk parameters that drive expected and unexpected loss: the Ausfallwahrscheinlichkeit (probability of default, PD), the Verlustquote bei Ausfall (loss given default, LGD), and the exposure \( \text{EAD} \). These linking models are the Satellitenmodelle (satellite models).

3.1 The PD satellite

The standard form is a logit relating the stressed point-in-time PD to macro factors. For a rating grade or segment \(g\):

$$ \operatorname{logit}\!\big(PD^{stress}_{g,t}\big)=\operatorname{logit}\!\big(PD^{TTC}_{g}\big)+\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\,\Delta x_{k,t} $$
where \(PD^{TTC}_{g}\) is the through-the-cycle anchor PD for grade \(g\); \(\Delta x_{k,t}\) is the deviation of macro factor \(k\) from baseline; \(\beta_k\) is the estimated sensitivity (e.g. \(\beta>0\) on unemployment); and \(\operatorname{logit}(p)=\ln\!\frac{p}{1-p}\).

The logit transform is not cosmetic: it keeps the stressed PD in \((0,1)\) for any shock magnitude, and it makes the macro effect multiplicative in the odds, which matches the empirical fact that a downturn hurts a weak grade proportionally more than a strong one. Solving back gives the stressed PD:

$$ PD^{stress}_{g,t}=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\big(-\operatorname{logit}(PD^{TTC}_{g})-\sum_k\beta_k\Delta x_{k,t}\big)} $$

Equivalently, in many sector implementations the effect is expressed as a Migrationsmatrix (migration matrix) shift: the adverse scenario raises downgrade probabilities and default rates simultaneously, so the whole rating distribution slides down. Module 12 (rating systems) and Module 18 (IRB parameter estimation) supply the \(PD^{TTC}\) anchors and the migration matrices this step perturbs.

3.2 The LGD satellite

LGD rises in a downturn for two reasons: collateral values fall (lower recoveries) and the population of defaulters worsens. The link is typically modelled on the Sicherheitenwert (collateral value), itself driven by property prices:

$$ LGD^{stress}=\min\!\Big(1,\;LGD^{base}\cdot\big(1+\delta\big)\Big),\qquad \delta=\gamma\cdot\big(-\Delta\text{HPI}\big) $$
where \(LGD^{base}\) is the baseline (or downturn-anchored) LGD; \(\Delta\text{HPI}\) is the property-price (Immobilienpreisindex) change, negative in stress; \(\gamma>0\) translates a price fall into an LGD uplift; and the \(\min(1,\cdot)\) cap enforces \(LGD\le 100\%\).

Note the CRR already requires a regulatory value downturn-LGD for IRB (Art. 181 CRR, with the estimation rules detailed in the EBA downturn-LGD guidelines/RTS) — see Module 18. The stress test pushes beyond the regulatory downturn floor into the scenario-specific tail; the trade-off is that a more conservative \(\gamma\) protects capital adequacy but, if asserted rather than estimated from the bank's own recovery data, invites a supervisory finding of unjustified prudence that distorts pricing and limits.

3.3 From parameters to capital — the two channels, done correctly

Two channels feed the capital ratio, and naming them precisely is what separates a methodology defender from a back-of-envelope modeller. The numerator (CET1) moves through provisioning; the denominator (RWA) moves through risk-weight migration.

Numerator: provisions, not raw EL

The first instinct — "deduct expected loss from capital" — is wrong twice over. What actually hits the profit-and-loss account, and through retained earnings the CET1, is the change in IFRS-9-Wertberichtigung (IFRS 9 expected-credit-loss provisions). IFRS 9 sorts exposures into stages: Stufe 1 (12-month ECL, performing), Stufe 2 (lifetime ECL after a significant increase in credit risk, SICR), and Stufe 3 (lifetime ECL, credit-impaired/defaulted). In a stress the dominant numerator mechanic is stage migration: a downgrade pushes a loan from Stufe 1 to Stufe 2, and the provision jumps from 12-month to lifetime ECL — a step-change far larger than the marginal PD move alone would suggest. This is the genuine P&L transmission channel in every modern ICAAP and EBA exercise.

$$ \Delta\text{CET1}^{P\&L} = -\big(ECL^{stress}-ECL^{base}\big)\,(1-\tau) $$
where \(ECL\) is the IFRS 9 lifetime/12-month expected credit loss aggregated over stages, and \(\tau\) is the tax shield. The accounting ECL — not the regulatory EL — is what flows through the numerator.
▶ Regulatory anchor — the IRB EL-shortfall

For IRB exposures there is a second, separate numerator effect that the raw-EL view misses entirely. CRR compares the regulatory erwarteter Verlust (expected loss, \(EL=PD\cdot LGD\cdot EAD\)) against accounting provisions. Where regulatory EL exceeds provisions, the EL-Fehlbetrag (EL shortfall) is deducted directly from CET1 (Art. 36(1)(d) and Art. 159 CRR); where provisions exceed regulatory EL, the excess may be added to Tier 2 up to a cap. So the CET1 hit is the provision-vs-regulatory-EL difference plus the provision charge itself, never a single raw-EL number applied twice. regulatory value A defender must keep three quantities distinct: accounting ECL (IFRS 9), regulatory EL (CRR), and the shortfall deduction that reconciles them.

Denominator: RWA migration

RWA itself moves because the IRB risk weight is a function of PD and LGD. The retail IRB capital requirement per unit of EAD is (Module 15):

$$ K=LGD\cdot N\!\Big(\tfrac{N^{-1}(PD)+\sqrt{R}\,N^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1-R}}\Big)-PD\cdot LGD,\qquad RWA = K\cdot 12.5 \cdot EAD $$
where \(R\) is the asset correlation, \(N(\cdot)\) the standard normal CDF and \(N^{-1}\) its inverse. Crucially, for retail exposures there is NO maturity-adjustment term (Art. 154 CRR). The factor \(\frac{1+(M-2.5)b}{1-1.5b}\) belongs to the corporate/institution/central-government formula (Art. 153 CRR) and must not be carried into a retail mortgage calculation.

The stressed capital ratio is then post-stress CET1 over post-stress RWA:

$$ \text{CET1 ratio}^{stress}=\frac{\text{CET1}_0-\sum_t \Delta ECL_t\,(1-\tau)-\Delta(\text{EL-shortfall})+\sum_t \text{retained earnings}_t}{RWA^{stress}} $$
where \(\Delta ECL_t\) is the incremental IFRS 9 provision vs baseline in year \(t\); the EL-shortfall term is the CRR deduction; \(\tau\) the tax shield; and \(RWA^{stress}\) reflects the migrated, higher-risk-weight portfolio.
⚠ The double-count / double-relief trap

A frequent error is to let the same loss reduce both the numerator (via provisions) and forget it raises the denominator (RWA via migration), or — worse — to net new provisions against RWA in a way that flatters the ratio. Stress accounting must be internally consistent. Note the interaction for defaulted exposures: a Stufe 3/defaulted IRB asset leaves the performing IRB risk-weight curve and is instead handled via best-estimate expected loss (BEEL) and the ELBE comparison — its UL contribution is \(\max(0, LGD^{default}-EL_{BE})\), and any positive part still attracts RWA. Under the standardised approach a defaulted exposure jumps to a 100% risk weight (or 150% where specific credit-risk adjustments are low). Get the sign discipline wrong and you report a more resilient bank under stress than at baseline, which no supervisor will believe.

4 · Worked example — an adverse shock end-to-end

▶ Setup

A Raiffeisenbank holds a homogeneous retail mortgage segment: \(EAD = €500\text{m}\), baseline \(PD^{TTC}=1.2\%\), baseline \(LGD=20\%\). Under Art. 154(3) CRR retail residential mortgages carry a fixed asset correlation \(R=0.15\) and — being retail — no maturity adjustment, so we use the pure retail \(K\) above (no \(M\)-term). Opening \(\text{CET1}=€90\text{m}\), opening total RWA \(=€750\text{m}\) (so opening CET1 ratio \(=12.0\%\)). To keep the arithmetic legible we treat the incremental provision as approximately the incremental regulatory EL (i.e. provisions track EL one-for-one and the EL-shortfall stays flat); in a real run the IFRS 9 stage-2 migration would make the numerator hit larger, not smaller. The adverse scenario: unemployment \(+4\) pp, property prices \(-20\%\) over the horizon.

4.1 Stress the PD

Suppose the PD satellite has been estimated with \(\beta_{u}=0.30\) per pp of unemployment (on the logit scale). Baseline logit: \(\operatorname{logit}(0.012)=\ln(0.012/0.988)=-4.412\). Add the shock \(0.30\times 4 = 1.20\): stressed logit \(=-3.212\). Invert: \(PD^{stress}=1/(1+e^{3.212})=1/(1+24.83)=3.87\%\). The PD has roughly tripled — consistent with a deep but not apocalyptic recession.

4.2 Stress the LGD

With \(\gamma=0.50\) and \(\Delta\text{HPI}=-20\%\): \(\delta=0.50\times 0.20=0.10\), so \(LGD^{stress}=20\%\times(1+0.10)=22\%\). (Many sector models would apply a steeper, non-linear haircut near the loan-to-value cliff; here we keep it linear for the arithmetic.)

4.3 Expected-loss / provision impact on CET1

$$ EL^{base}=0.012\times0.20\times500=€1.20\text{m},\qquad EL^{stress}=0.0387\times0.22\times500=€4.26\text{m} $$

Incremental EL \(=4.26-1.20=€3.06\text{m}\). Read this as the incremental provision flowing through P&L (the simplification above). Ignoring tax and earnings offset for the conservative read, CET1 falls to \(90-3.06=€86.94\text{m}\). In a full model the Stufe 1→Stufe 2 lifetime-ECL jump would push this hit higher.

4.4 RWA migration

Plug stressed parameters into the retail IRB \(K\). With \(PD=3.87\%\), \(LGD=22\%\), \(R=0.15\): the conditional default term is \(N\!\big(\tfrac{N^{-1}(0.0387)+\sqrt{0.15}\,N^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{0.85}}\big)\). Here \(N^{-1}(0.0387)=-1.766\), \(N^{-1}(0.999)=3.090\), \(\sqrt{0.15}=0.387\), \(\sqrt{0.85}=0.922\). Numerator \(=-1.766+0.387\times3.090=-1.766+1.196=-0.570\); divided by \(0.922=-0.618\); \(N(-0.618)=0.268\). So \(K=0.22\times0.268-0.0387\times0.22=0.0590-0.0085=0.0505\), i.e. \(5.05\%\). Then \(RWA^{stress}_{seg}=0.0505\times12.5\times500=€315.6\text{m}\).

The baseline \(K\) (\(PD=1.2\%\), \(LGD=20\%\)): \(N^{-1}(0.012)=-2.257\); numerator \(=-2.257+1.196=-1.061\); \(/0.922=-1.151\); \(N(-1.151)=0.125\); \(K=0.20\times0.125-0.012\times0.20=0.0250-0.0024=0.0226\); \(RWA^{base}_{seg}=0.0226\times12.5\times500=€141.3\text{m}\). The segment's RWA more than doubles: \(\Delta RWA=315.6-141.3=€174.3\text{m}\).

4.5 Read the post-stress ratio

New total RWA \(=750+174.3=€924.3\text{m}\). Post-stress CET1 ratio:

$$ \text{CET1 ratio}^{stress}=\frac{86.94}{924.3}=9.41\% $$
▶ Reading it — decomposing the draw-down correctly

The ratio fell from 12.0% to 9.41% — a draw-down of 2.59 pp. To attribute it, decompose channel by channel. EL/provision channel (apply the numerator hit first, RWA still at €750m): \(86.94/750 = 11.59\%\), so the EL channel costs \(12.00-11.59 = 0.41\) pp \(\approx\) 16% of the fall. RWA channel (now apply the migrated denominator): \(86.94/924.3 = 9.41\%\), so the RWA channel costs \(11.59-9.41 = 2.18\) pp \(\approx\) 84%. The split is therefore roughly five-sixths denominator / one-sixth numerator — RWA migration overwhelmingly dominates. The exam point survives in stronger form: "stress = provisions" understates the hit drastically, because the migration channel is here about five times larger than the loss channel.

▶ Against which hurdle? The stacking order and the MDA

A post-stress 9.41% is meaningless until measured against the right ladder. CET1 requirements stack: 4.5% Pillar 1 minimum, plus the CET1 portion of the P2R (Pillar 2 requirement, binding), plus the kombinierter Kapitalpuffer (combined buffer — CCB + any countercyclical/systemic/sector buffers, Module 16), and finally P2G (Pillar 2 guidance) sitting on top of everything. P2G is non-binding and has no mechanical formula linking it to a stress-test result — it is supervisory judgement informed by, but not computed from, the EU-wide test's maximum draw-down. The decisive trip-wire is the combined buffer: breach it and Art. 141 CRD / §24 BWG impose the MDA (Maximum Distributable Amount, maximaler ausschüttungsfähiger Betrag), automatically capping dividends, AT1 coupons and variable remuneration. So the post-stress read management must defend is not "are we above 4.5%?" but "does the trajectory dip into the buffer and arm the MDA brake?"

5 · In practice — the sector and the EU-wide test

5.1 The EU-wide EBA stress test mechanics

The biennial EU-wide exercise coordinated by the EBA is a constrained bottom-up test (the cadence and framework are under active review by the EBA, so confirm the current schedule). Its defining features:

FeatureMechanicWhy
Balance sheetStatische Bilanz (static): assets that mature are replaced like-for-like; no management actions, no deleveragingComparability across banks; removes the temptation to "model your way out"
ScenariosCommon baseline + single severe adverses Szenario over a 3-year horizon, designed by the ESRB/ECBA level playing field of severity
ConstraintsCaps, floors and methodological notes (e.g. no PD/LGD improvement vs starting point under adverse)Prevents optimistic satellite models from understating losses
MetricCET1 ratio trajectory and the maximum draw-down to the trough yearThe trough, not the endpoint, reveals fragility
QAQualitätssicherung: supervisors challenge submissions against benchmarks; outliers must explainThe numbers are only as good as the challenge
▶ Regulatory anchor — no mechanical pass/fail

The EU-wide test is regulatory value in design (the ESRB scenario, the constraints, the templates are mandated) but its output is not a pass/fail capital requirement. There is no automatic CET1 hurdle that fails a bank, and — critically — no formula maps the trough draw-down to a P2G number. The result is a qualitative-plus-quantitative input into the SREP and the supervisor's judgement-based setting of P2G — see Module 21. Contrast this with an institution's own ICAAP stress test, which uses a ÖRS modelling decision dynamic balance sheet and management actions, because its purpose is realistic self-assessment, not cross-bank comparison. The trade-off: static comparability buys you a clean league table at the cost of realism; dynamic ICAAP buys realism at the cost of comparability and the risk of self-serving assumptions.

5.2 The sector approach and the Deckungsmasse

Most Raiffeisen primary banks are too small to run a full bottom-up satellite suite alone, so the Sektor-Risikocontrolling (sector risk controlling) supplies a common scenario set and centrally estimated satellite parameters that each bank applies to its own portfolio — pure proportionality under ÖRS modelling decision. This guarantees a coherent, comparable stress across the Verbund while letting each Institut reflect its own exposure mix. The trade-off: centrally estimated \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) may not fit an atypical local portfolio (e.g. a bank heavy in tourism or agriculture), so the Leitfaden expects a local plausibility overlay.

▶ ÖRS / cover-pool point

For institutions issuing Pfandbriefe (covered bonds), the stress also bites the Deckungsmasse (cover pool): adverse property prices erode the over-collateralisation as the eligible value of pledged mortgages is haircut. The Leitfaden's stress approach therefore tests not only the bank-level CET1 ratio but the Überdeckung (over-collateralisation) of the pool — a separate early-warning trigger, because cover-pool deterioration can force asset top-ups precisely when capital is scarcest. This is the liquidity/funding tail that Module 23 picks up.

6 · Reverse stress testing — nuances & traps

6.1 Methodology: invert the chain

A Reverse Stresstest fixes the endpoint — the bank is non-viable — and searches for the scenario(s) that produce it. "Non-viable" must be defined operationally: typically the CET1 ratio breaching a critical threshold (e.g. falling below the point where the business model is no longer fundable), a liquidity exhaustion point, or the loss of market confidence. The method runs the satellite chain backward: from a target capital depletion, solve for the loss, then for the PD/LGD level, then for the macro path consistent with it.

▶ Inverting our example

Suppose non-viability is a CET1 ratio of 5.0%. Holding RWA migration as in §4.4 (\(RWA^{stress}\approx€924\text{m}\)), the maximum tolerable CET1 is \(0.05\times924=€46.2\text{m}\), i.e. a depletion of \(90-46.2=€43.8\text{m}\) from provisions. With \(LGD^{stress}=22\%\) and \(EAD=€500\text{m}\), the implied stressed PD solves \(43.8 = PD\times0.22\times500 - EL^{base}\), giving cumulative EL \(\approx€45.0\text{m}\) and \(PD^{stress}\approx 41\%\) over the horizon (recognising this would also re-migrate RWA further — the real solution iterates). Inverting the PD satellite, \(\operatorname{logit}(0.41)-\operatorname{logit}(0.012)=(-0.36)-(-4.41)=4.05\); at \(\beta_u=0.30\) that needs an unemployment shock of \(\approx 13.5\) pp. The governance output: "we only break if unemployment rises by ~13–14 pp on this segment alone" — a scenario management can judge as remote or, alarmingly, not.

6.2 Governance use

▶ Why reverse stress testing is a board instrument

The deliverable is not a number but a narrative of failure: the named scenarios, their plausibility, and the early-warning indicators that would signal you are on that path. The EBA/GL/2018/04 Guidelines require institutions to run reverse stress tests; the linkage of those failure scenarios into the recovery plan (the Sanierungsplan) flows principally from the BRRD recovery-planning regime and the EBA recovery-plan guidelines — the failure scenarios become the triggers and the recovery options become the answer. This is its governance dignity: it links risk identification to a pre-agreed playbook, so the Aufsichtsrat has decided before the crisis what would count as the crisis. Module 24 (resolution) inherits these failure scenarios as the entry point to the gone-concern world.

6.3 Traps

⚠ Calibration and coherence traps

Asserted sensitivities. A \(\beta\) or \(\gamma\) pulled from "expert judgement" without back-testing against the bank's own default and recovery history is the most common finding — it makes the stress neither plausible nor defensible. Expert overlays are legitimate, but only as adjustments to a statistically estimated base, with the rationale documented.

EL is not a P&L charge. Treating regulatory \(PD\cdot LGD\cdot EAD\) as if it lands directly in the income statement skips the entire IFRS 9 ECL / EL-shortfall machinery of §3.3. The numerator moves via provisions and the CET1 shortfall deduction, not via raw EL.

Linear extrapolation into the tail. Satellite models estimated on mild cycles understate non-linearities (collateral cliffs, correlation rising in crisis). A model that is well-behaved at −5% GDP may badly under-predict at −12%.

Single-factor blindness. Running only sensitivity analyses misses the second-round effects — rising rates and falling property prices and rising unemployment reinforce each other; the joint move is worse than the sum of marginals.

Survivorship in scenario design. Forward scenarios are imagined by people who run the bank; they tend to test survivable worlds. Reverse stress testing exists precisely to break that anchoring — do not let it degrade into "the adverse scenario, but a bit worse."

Static-vs-dynamic confusion. Importing the EBA static-balance-sheet constraint into your ICAAP (or vice versa) mislabels the test's purpose and will draw a supervisory question. Know which instrument you are running and why.

Recap

  • Stress testing is the forward-looking complement to point-in-time Pillar 1 capital; it powers the ICAAP/RTFA Extremfall and is required by CRD/BWG, EBA/GL/2018/04 and ECB expectations.
  • Three instruments answer three questions: sensitivity (one factor), scenario (many factors, multi-year, baseline vs adverse), and reverse stress testing (fix non-viability, find the scenario).
  • The transmission chain runs macro → satellite models → stressed PD (logit shift) and LGD (collateral haircut). The numerator moves via IFRS 9 ECL stage migration (Stufe 1→2 lifetime-ECL jumps) and the IRB EL-shortfall deduction — NOT raw EL — while the denominator moves via RWA migration; both must be counted consistently.
  • In the worked example CET1 fell from 12.0% to 9.41% (a 2.59 pp draw-down). Decomposed correctly the RWA channel is ~84% (≈2.18 pp) and the EL/provision channel only ~16% (≈0.41 pp) — roughly five-sixths denominator, one-sixth numerator. 'Stress = provisions' understates the hit drastically.
  • A post-stress ratio is read against the stack P1 (4.5%) + P2R + combined buffer + P2G; P2G is non-binding with no mechanical link to the stress result, and breaching the combined buffer arms the MDA (Art. 141 CRD / §24 BWG) restricting distributions.
  • The EU-wide EBA test is static-balance-sheet, common-adverse-scenario, trough-CET1, QA-challenged, and feeds SREP/P2G with no pass/fail and no formula to P2G — deliberately different from a dynamic, management-action ICAAP test. Reverse stress testing inverts the chain to a failure narrative feeding the Sanierungsplan (via the BRRD recovery regime) and the Deckungsmasse over-collateralisation test.
🇦🇹 Zusammenfassung (Deutsch)
  • Stresstests ergänzen die statische Säule-1-Sicht um eine vorausschauende Perspektive und speisen den Extremfall der Risikotragfähigkeit (ICAAP).
  • Drei Instrumente: Sensitivitätsanalyse (ein Faktor), Szenarioanalyse (mehrere Faktoren, mehrjährig, Basis vs. advers) und Reverse Stresstest (vom Ausfall rückwärts zum Szenario).
  • Der Kapitaleffekt läuft über zwei Kanäle: der Zähler über die IFRS-9-Wertberichtigung (Stufenmigration 1→2, Lifetime-ECL) und den EL-Fehlbetrag-Abzug (Art. 36/159 CRR) — nicht über den rohen EL — der Nenner über die RWA-Migration.
  • Im Beispiel fiel die CET1-Quote von 12,0% auf 9,41% (−2,59 pp); korrekt zerlegt entfallen ~84% auf den RWA-Kanal und nur ~16% auf den Wertberichtigungs-Kanal (rund fünf Sechstel Nenner, ein Sechstel Zähler).
  • Die Quote wird gegen den Stapel P1 + P2R + kombinierter Puffer + P2G gelesen; P2G ist unverbindlich und nicht mechanisch aus dem Stresstest abgeleitet, und ein Pufferunterschreiten löst die MDA (Art. 141 CRD / §24 BWG) aus.
  • Der EU-weite EBA-Stresstest ist statisch und vergleichbar (kein hartes Bestehen/Durchfallen, keine Formel zu P2G); der Reverse Stresstest liefert ein Ausfallszenario für den Sanierungsplan (über das BRRD-Sanierungsregime), die Deckungsmasse-Überdeckung ist ein zusätzlicher Frühwarnauslöser.
