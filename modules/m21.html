Module 21 — The EBA & ECB map: ICAAP/ILAAP, SREP, NPE & calendar provisioning, large exposures, outsourcing

The CRR gives you Pillar 1 — a mechanical, risk-weighted minimum. But the regulation that actually shapes a Raiffeisen bank's day, and the conversations you will have with the FMA and the OeNB, lives in soft law: ECB Guides, EBA Guidelines, and a thin layer of CRR articles that turn supervisory expectation into a hard deduction. This module is the map. It walks the ICAAP/ILAAP self-assessment, the SREP that grades it, the NPE machinery and the prudential backstop that silently eats capital on stale defaults, the large-exposures regime that the R-IPS (Raiffeisen institutsbezogenes Sicherungssystem, institutional protection scheme) partly switches off, and the outsourcing and ICT governance overlay. For each instrument: name it, state what it forces, and show how the Leitfaden and the sector satisfy it — separating what the CRR commands from what proportionality lets the sector choose.

By the end of this module you can…

  • Distinguish the economic and normative internal perspectives of the ICAAP and tie each to the Extremfall/Problemfall scenarios of Modules 3–4.
  • Read a SREP score: name the four elements, explain how P2R (binding) and P2G (guidance, bucketed off stress-test depletion) are set, and stack them correctly on Pillar 1 plus the combined buffer to derive the MDA trigger.
  • Apply the calendar-provisioning backstop of CRR Art. 47a–47c: classify an exposure as unsecured / movable-secured / immovable-secured, check the 26 April 2019 origination cut-off, read the correct vintage factor, and compute the CET1 deduction.
  • State the large-exposures 25%-of-Tier-1 limit, identify connected clients via control OR economic interdependence, and explain why the R-IPS exemption matters for intra-sector exposures.
  • Locate outsourcing (EBA/GL/2019/02), DORA/ICT and internal governance in the supervisory stack and say what each forces the sector to document.
  • For any fixed number, decide whether it is a regulatory value the CRR forces or an ÖRS modelling decision taken under proportionality — and name the trade-off.

1 · Motivation — why this exists

By now you can compute a risk-weighted asset and read a capital ratio (Modules 1–2), and you can build the Extremfall/Problemfall scenarios the Leitfaden's Früherkennung leans on (Modules 3–4). All of that is Pillar 1: a single, harmonised, mechanical minimum. If Pillar 1 were the whole story, supervision would be an arithmetic check and a Junior Risk Controller would be a calculator with a chair.

It is not the whole story, for one structural reason. Pillar 1 is deliberately blunt. It uses standardised risk weights (or model outputs disciplined by floors) that cannot see your concentration in Tyrolean tourism, your interest-rate risk in the banking book, your reliance on one core-banking provider, or the fact that a third of your defaulted book has been sitting unprovisioned for four years. Pillar 2 — the supervisory review — exists precisely to catch what Pillar 1 cannot. And because the euro area runs a Single Supervisory Mechanism, the way Pillar 2 is operated is written down in a stack of ECB Guides and EBA Guidelines that are not regulations but bind in practice.

▶ The core idea in one sentence

The CRR sets the floor; the supervisory soft law decides how much you must hold above the floor, how stale defaults are forced into provisions, who counts as one borrower, and what you must document about the people and machines running the bank. Pillar 1 is law you compute; Pillar 2 is law you must defend.

For the Raiffeisen sector the stakes are concrete. A small primary bank cannot staff an ICAAP function the size of a G-SIB's, so proportionality is not a courtesy — it is the only way the regime is survivable. The sector solves this by mutualising: Sector Risk Controlling Services builds the methodology centrally, the R-IPS pools the prudential risk, and each primary bank inherits a defensible framework it could never build alone. Your job is to understand that framework well enough to derive it and defend it to your own Aufsichtsrat — not merely to run it.

2 · Concept & logic — the supervisory stack

Think of the regime as four layers, from hardest to softest law, each wrapping the one below.

▶ Why the distinction bites

P2R is binding: breach it and you breach a capital requirement, with the same MDA (Maximum Distributable Amount) consequences as a Pillar-1 breach. P2G is guidance: sit below it and you do not breach a requirement, but you invite intensified supervision and lose the supervisor's goodwill. The entire economic-vs-normative ICAAP architecture exists to evidence that you can satisfy both, today and through stress.

The "comply or explain" status of EBA Guidelines deserves a line. Under Art. 16 of the EBA Regulation, competent authorities (the FMA for less significant institutions; the ECB for significant ones) must notify whether they comply. The FMA has, in practice, adopted the relevant guidelines, so for an Austrian Raiffeisenbank an EBA Guideline is effectively mandatory even though it is formally soft law. Treat "the EBA recommends" as "the FMA expects."

3 · Derivation — building each instrument from its purpose

3.1 ICAAP — the economic and normative perspectives

The Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) is required by Art. 73 CRD: an institution must have "sound, effective and comprehensive strategies and processes to assess and maintain… the amounts, types and distribution of internal capital… adequate to cover the nature and level of the risks." The ECB's Guide to the ICAAP operationalises this around seven principles and, crucially, two perspectives that you must run in parallel.

$$ \text{Adequacy} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \underbrace{C_{\text{internal}}}_{\text{available internal capital}} \;\ge\; \underbrace{\sum_i \text{EC}_i}_{\text{economic capital, all material risks}} \quad\text{(economic)} $$
where \( \text{EC}_i \) is the economic capital for risk type \(i\) — credit, market, operational, IRRBB, concentration, business — measured at the institution's chosen confidence level over a one-year horizon, and \( C_{\text{internal}} \) is an internal (not regulatory) notion of loss-absorbing capacity.

That is the economic perspective (ökonomische Perspektive): a present-value, gone-concern-flavoured view that ignores regulatory accounting conventions and asks "could the bank absorb the unexpected losses across all material risks?" It is the natural home of your Extremfall from Module 3 — a severe-but-plausible tail where you test whether internal capital survives a stacked shock.

$$ \min_{t\in\{0,1,2,3\}} \; \text{CET1 ratio}_t^{\text{(adverse)}} \;\ge\; \text{OCR}_t \quad\text{(normative)} $$
where OCR (Overall Capital Requirement) = Pillar 1 (8%) + P2R + combined buffer requirement, projected over a (typically) 3-year horizon under both a baseline and an adverse path; the adverse path is your Problemfall. The normative perspective tests survival of all binding requirements; P2G is an additional supervisory expectation layered above the OCR, not itself a hard limit.

That is the normative perspective (normative Perspektive): a multi-year, going-concern projection of the actual regulatory ratios under baseline and adverse macro scenarios, checking that you stay above all regulatory requirements throughout (and ideally above P2G). The two perspectives inform each other — a risk that is material economically must be reflected in the normative projection, and vice versa.

▶ ÖRS modelling choice — where the sector exercises judgement

The confidence level for the economic perspective is an ÖRS modelling decision: the ECB Guide does not mandate a number, only that it be "consistent with a high level of solvency." Many institutions anchor on a confidence level such as 99.9% over one year to align with the IRB asymptotic-single-risk-factor calibration (Module 12). Trade-off: a higher confidence level is easier to defend to the supervisor but consumes more economic capital and can make the bank look constrained; a lower one frees capital but invites the challenge "why are you less prudent than your A-IRB Pillar-1 calibration?" The sector picks one number centrally so that every primary bank is consistent and the choice is defensible once, not 300 times.

3.2 ILAAP — the liquidity twin

The Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP), required by Art. 86 CRD and detailed in the ECB Guide to the ILAAP, is the same machinery for liquidity and funding. It runs its own economic perspective (survival horizon under a liquidity stress, the internal counterpart to the LCR of Module 22) and a normative perspective (projecting LCR and NSFR through stress). The deliverable that ties ICAAP and ILAAP together for the supervisor is the Capital Adequacy Statement and its liquidity sibling — a board-signed letter, typically annual, in which the management body explicitly attests that capital (resp. liquidity) is adequate, names the key assumptions, and owns the conclusion. It is short, and it is the single most scrutinised page, because a signed attestation converts a technical exercise into personal accountability of the Geschäftsleitung.

3.3 SREP — how the supervisor grades it

The Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP) is the supervisor's annual verdict, structured by the EBA SREP Guidelines (EBA/GL/2022/03, which revised the original 2014 guidelines). It assesses four elements, each scored 1 (low risk) to 4 (high risk):

  1. Business model analysis (BMA) — is the strategy viable over 12 months and sustainable over 3 years? For a small Raiffeisenbank, the standing question is interest-margin dependence and cost-income pressure.
  2. Internal governance and institution-wide controls — the three lines of defence, risk culture, the quality of the ICAAP/ILAAP itself.
  3. Capital adequacy (risks to capital) — credit, market, operational, IRRBB and concentration risk, and the ICAAP's credibility. This element sizes P2R.
  4. Liquidity and funding adequacy — the ILAAP's credibility; sizes any liquidity-specific Pillar-2 add-on.

The scores feed a holistic judgement, which produces (i) P2R — a binding own-funds requirement covering risks under- or un-covered by Pillar 1, set as a percentage of TREA; (ii) P2G — a non-binding expectation sitting above the buffers; and (iii) qualitative measures. Since 2021, P2R need not be met entirely with CET1: it can be partially met with AT1 and T2 (the "Pillar 2 composition" rule of Art. 104a CRD), mirroring the 56.25% CET1 / 75% T1 split of Pillar 1.

▶ CRD point — P2G is set by bucketing stress-test depletion

Under the SSM/EBA approach, P2G is no longer a raw read-off of the single worst CET1 depletion number. The supervisor maps the maximum CET1 depletion observed in the relevant stress test to a set of buckets, each bucket carrying an indicative P2G range, and then applies supervisory judgement within (or, exceptionally, outside) the range. A controller defending the figure must be able to say: "P2G is bucketed off the adverse-scenario CET1 depletion, not mechanically equal to it." The exact bucket boundaries and ranges are a live, periodically-recalibrated supervisory parameter — flag them to verify rather than quote from memory.

▶ CRD point — the stacking order is law, not convention

Art. 104a CRD requires P2R to be additional own funds for risks not (or not adequately) covered by Pillar 1; Art. 104b governs P2G; Art. 141 makes the combined buffer requirement the trigger for MDA restrictions. The order matters: buffers sit on top of Pillar 1 + P2R, and P2G sits on top of the buffers. Eating into P2G is permitted; eating into the buffer is not free.

3.4 The stacking, derived

The Overall Capital Requirement and the MDA trigger follow mechanically once you accept the order. Let everything be a percentage of TREA:

$$ \text{TSCR} = \underbrace{8\%}_{\text{P1}} + \text{P2R}, \qquad \text{OCR} = \text{TSCR} + \text{CBR}, \qquad \text{MDA trigger}_{\text{CET1}} = 4.5\% + \text{P2R}_{\text{CET1}} + \text{CBR} $$
where TSCR = Total SREP Capital Requirement, CBR = combined buffer requirement, and \( \text{P2R}_{\text{CET1}} \) is the CET1-eligible portion of P2R. Below the MDA trigger, distributions (dividends, AT1 coupons, variable remuneration) are capped by the Art. 141 distribution ladder.
▶ CRD point — the SyRB now generally stacks, it is no longer "the higher of"

It is tempting to write the combined buffer as CCB + CCyB + "the higher of G-SII/O-SII/systemic-risk buffer." That was the pre-CRD-V logic. Since CRD V the systemic risk buffer (SyRB) is generally additive to the O-SII/G-SII buffer rather than capped against it — the "higher of" capping was largely removed. So for the cases the CRD now specifies:

$$ \text{CBR} = \text{CCB} + \text{CCyB}_{\text{inst}} + \max(\text{G-SII},\,\text{O-SII}) + \text{SyRB} $$
where \(\text{CCyB}_{\text{inst}}\) is the institution-specific countercyclical rate, \(\max(\text{G-SII},\text{O-SII})\) still takes the higher of the two global/other systemically-important buffers, and the SyRB is added on top (subject to the authorisation thresholds the CRD sets). For a primary Raiffeisenbank the G-SII/O-SII buffers are typically zero, so in practice CBR ≈ CCB + CCyB (+ any applicable SyRB). The exact buffer rates are live and macroprudentially set — flag them to verify.

3.5 NPE management and the prudential backstop

Two separate things share the word "NPE" and you must never conflate them. First, the EBA Guidelines on management of non-performing and forborne exposures (EBA/GL/2018/06) are process law: any bank with a gross NPL ratio at or above a threshold (the guidelines use 5%) must build an NPE strategy, an operational reduction plan, governance, and forbearance policy. NPE itself is defined via the ITS on supervisory reporting: 90-days-past-due or unlikely-to-pay (UTP), with cure periods; forbearance (FBE — forborne exposure) tags concessions granted to a borrower in financial difficulty and carries its own probation periods before a forborne NPE can re-perform.

Second — and quite distinct — the prudential backstop or calendar provisioning is capital law, hard-wired into the CRR by Art. 47a–47c. It does not care about your accounting provisions' adequacy in principle; it imposes a minimum coverage on each non-performing exposure that ramps up with the time since classification, and any shortfall is deducted directly from CET1 under Art. 36(1)(m).

▶ CRR point — the backstop bites only on exposures created from 26 April 2019

The hard Pillar-1 backstop of Art. 47a–47c applies in full only to exposures originated/created on or after 26 April 2019. Exposures created before that date fall outside the Art. 47a–47c deduction entirely — the legacy stock is governed instead by the ECB Addendum / SREP expectation (Pillar 2), not by the automatic CET1 deduction. A defender must state this plainly: the first question on any NPE is not just "how old is the default?" but "when was the exposure created?" Pre-cut-off exposures simply are not subject to the hard deduction.

3.5.1 The three ramps — different tracks, different end-points

Art. 47c sets three coverage ramps, because the speed with which a recovery is plausible depends on what stands behind the loan. The factor is applied to the relevant portion of the exposure (the secured portion to the secured ramp, the unsecured portion to the unsecured ramp), and the shortfall is the required coverage less eligible coverage.

⚠ Trap — two regimes, one word

The ECB published its Addendum to the NPL Guidance (a supervisory expectation, Pillar 2) before the co-legislators enacted Art. 47a–47c (Pillar 1, a hard CET1 deduction). They overlap but are not identical and apply to different vintages — the Addendum governs the pre-26-April-2019 legacy stock, the CRR backstop governs exposures created from that date. In a viva, if you are asked "is calendar provisioning Pillar 1 or Pillar 2?", the correct answer is: the CRR backstop is Pillar 1 since the 2019 amendment and applies to exposures created from 26 April 2019; the ECB Addendum is the Pillar-2 expectation for older exposures. Quote the wrong one and you have failed the question.

3.6 Large exposures

CRR Part Four (Art. 387–403) caps concentration to a single counterparty. The core limit:

$$ E^{\ast}_{j} \;=\; \text{EAD}_j - \text{CRM}_j \;\le\; 25\% \times \text{T1} $$
where \( E^{\ast}_{j} \) is the exposure value to client or group of connected clients \(j\) after eligible credit-risk mitigation and exemptions, and T1 is Tier-1 capital (since CRR II the base is Tier 1, not total own funds). A lower absolute floor of EUR 150m applies to certain G-SII-to-G-SII exposures — not your concern in the primary sector, but know it exists.

"Connected clients" (Gruppe verbundener Kunden) is the subtle part, governed by EBA Guidelines on connected clients (EBA/GL/2017/15): two clients are grouped if there is a control relationship or an economic interdependence — where the financial difficulty of one would likely cause difficulty for the other (e.g. a single dominant customer or supplier, a common funding source, or a guarantor whose failure would propagate). The guidelines give indicative reference points as examples (such as around 50% of gross receipts coming from, or going to, a single counterparty), not bright-line triggers — you must look through legal form to economic reality, which is exactly the discipline the Früherkennung trains.

3.7 The R-IPS treatment

Intra-sector exposures are where the Raiffeisen structure rewrites the rule. Under Art. 113(7) CRR, exposures to members of the same institutional protection scheme can, with supervisory permission, receive a 0% risk weight in Pillar 1; and under Art. 400(2)(d)/(g) and the national discretion in the BWG, intra-IPS exposures may be exempted from the large-exposures limit. Economically this is correct: an exposure of one Raiffeisenbank to another inside the same protection scheme is not the same concentration risk as an exposure to an unrelated obligor, because the scheme stands behind both.

▶ ÖRS modelling choice — the price of the exemption

The 0% weight and the large-exposures exemption are regulatory values in the sense that the CRR grants them — but only if the IPS meets the conditions of Art. 113(7): a contractual or statutory liability arrangement, a common risk-monitoring system, and its own consolidated/aggregated risk reporting. Building and running that monitoring system is the ÖRS modelling decision. Trade-off: the sector accepts a heavy, centralised early-warning and risk-aggregation apparatus (the very Früherkennung Leitfaden this course teaches) in exchange for the capital relief. Lose the apparatus and you lose the exemption — the soft-law obligation is the consideration for the hard-law benefit.

3.8 Outsourcing, DORA, and internal governance

The EBA Guidelines on outsourcing arrangements (EBA/GL/2019/02) require an outsourcing register, materiality/criticality assessment, due diligence, exit strategies, and — for critical or important functions — that the institution retains the competence to oversee the provider and the supervisor retains access rights. For the sector, the core-banking platform, much of IT, and parts of risk reporting are outsourced within the sector (to shared service entities), which is operationally efficient but does not dissolve the responsibility: each primary bank's management body remains accountable. Layered on top is DORA (the Digital Operational Resilience Act, Reg. 2022/2554, applicable from 17 January 2025), which hardens ICT risk management, incident reporting, resilience testing, and third-party (ICT) oversight into directly-applicable EU law. Internal governance — the EBA Guidelines on internal governance and the fit-and-proper regime — closes the loop: the people are assessed, the functions are independent, and the three lines of defence are real.

4 · Worked example — calendar provisioning bites a primary bank

▶ Setup

A Raiffeisenbank has a commercial loan of EUR 1,000,000, created in 2021 (so squarely inside the post-26-April-2019 Art. 47a–47c backstop), that was classified non-performing during its fourth year following classification — i.e. we read the "after 4 years" column. EUR 600,000 of it is secured by a registered mortgage on commercial real estate (immovable collateral); EUR 400,000 is unsecured. Accounting provisions (IFRS 9 stage 3) already booked: EUR 250,000. The bank's Tier-1 capital is EUR 30,000,000. We compute the CET1 deduction the prudential backstop forces, and check the large-exposures headroom on the same name. Every output figure below is flagged to verify because it is only as correct as the Art. 47c factors it rests on.

Step 1 — split by collateral, read both legs from the SAME year-4 column. This is the discipline the previous draft got wrong: at a year-4 vintage the unsecured leg is at 1.00 (full cover from year 3) and the immovable-secured leg is at 0.25 (the year-4 factor), not 0.35 (which is the year-5 immovable factor).

PortionGrossBackstop factor at "after 4 yrs"Required minimum coverage
Unsecured400,000100%400,000
Secured (immovable)600,00025%150,000
Total required1,000,000—550,000

Step 2 — compare to actual coverage. The backstop credits your existing accounting provisions (and certain other adjustments) against the requirement. Shortfall:

$$ \text{Shortfall} = \max\!\big(0,\; \text{required minimum} - \text{eligible coverage}\big) = \max(0,\; 550{,}000 - 250{,}000) = \mathbf{300{,}000} $$
where eligible coverage here is the IFRS 9 stage-3 provision of EUR 250,000; in a full computation you would also net specific credit-risk adjustments and any additional value adjustments per Art. 47c. The EUR 300,000 result is only as reliable as the 0.25 year-4 factor.

Step 3 — deduct from CET1. EUR 300,000 comes straight off CET1 under Art. 36(1)(m). Note what just happened: the bank's accounting P&L did not move, but its regulatory capital fell by EUR 300,000 purely because the default is old. The backstop is a clock, not a credit assessment.

Step 4 — the same name against the large-exposures limit. The post-CRM exposure value (say EUR 1,000,000 gross, less EUR 600,000 eligible immovable CRM treated under the LE rules, leaving roughly EUR 400,000 — exact treatment depends on the CRM method) is trivially below \(25\% \times \text{T1} = 0.25 \times 30{,}000{,}000 = \text{EUR } 7{,}500{,}000\). No LE breach. But observe that the Tier-1 base for that 25% limit is after the deduction — the backstop quietly tightens your large-exposures headroom too: \(0.25 \times (30{,}000{,}000 - 300{,}000) = \) EUR 7,425,000, a EUR 75,000 shave off every concentration limit.

▶ The lesson the arithmetic teaches

One stale loan triggered a EUR 300,000 CET1 deduction and shaved EUR 75,000 off every concentration limit in the book. (Had this leg been movable-secured at year 4 the factor would be steeper still — 0.55 vintage — so collateral class, not just age, drives the bill.) This is why the EBA/GL/2018/06 reduction plan and the Art. 47a–47c backstop are two sides of one coin: the cheapest way to avoid the backstop is to resolve or write down NPEs before the calendar runs — exactly what the NPE strategy forces you to plan.

6 · Nuances & traps

⚠ P2R vs P2G — the breach asymmetry

Falling below P2R is a requirement breach with Art. 141 MDA consequences; falling into P2G is not. Candidates routinely stack P2G under the buffer or treat it as binding. It sits above the combined buffer and is guidance — breaching it typically triggers intensified supervision and a dialogue (a remediation/recovery conversation), not an automatic distribution restriction. Get the order in §3.4 exactly right.

⚠ "Economic" ≠ "internal model"

The economic perspective of the ICAAP is not the IRB approach. It is an internal, present-value view that may use your own confidence level and your own risk taxonomy. Conflating it with regulatory IRB outputs (Module 12) is a classic error — they can use the same engines but answer different questions.

⚠ The backstop has THREE tracks, a vintage, AND an origination gate — and it is a clock

Three things must be checked before you deduct a cent. (1) Origination date: if the exposure was created before 26 April 2019, the Art. 47a–47c deduction does not apply at all — it is the ECB Addendum's Pillar-2 world. (2) Collateral track: unsecured (full cover by year 3), movable/other-secured (full cover by year 8), immovable/eligible-guarantee (full cover from year 10). Read both legs of a split exposure from the same year column — that was the error that propagated through the previous draft, where a year-4 secured leg was wrongly read at the year-5 factor. (3) Quality is irrelevant: a well-collateralised, recoverable loan still gets deducted if it is old enough. Always flag the ramp factors as to-verify — the secured/unsecured/movable percentages by year are the single most-misquoted figures in this whole module.

⚠ The CBR is mostly additive now, not "higher of"

Since CRD V the SyRB generally stacks on top of the O-SII/G-SII buffer rather than being capped against it. Writing the combined buffer as "CCB + CCyB + the higher of all systemic buffers" is the old, pre-CRD-V picture and will mis-state the MDA trigger. Take \(\max(\text{G-SII},\text{O-SII})\) and then add the SyRB, per §3.4.

⚠ IPS exemption is conditional, not automatic

The 0% risk weight and large-exposures exemption depend on continuously meeting Art. 113(7). They are not a birthright of the Raiffeisen brand; they are paid for with the risk-monitoring apparatus. If the sector's early-warning system degrades, the prudential foundation of the exemption degrades with it — which is, ultimately, why this entire course exists.

⚠ Soft law is not optional law

"Comply or explain" sounds discretionary. For Austrian Raiffeisen banks under FMA supervision that has adopted the relevant EBA Guidelines, treat every cited Guideline as binding expectation. The only safe posture in front of the Aufsichtsrat is: name the instrument, state the requirement, evidence compliance.

Recap

  • The supervisory regime is a four-layer stack: CRR Pillar 1 → CRD buffers → Pillar 2 (SREP-set P2R binding + P2G guidance) → conduct-of-business EBA Guidelines and DORA. EBA Guidelines are 'comply or explain' but effectively binding once the FMA adopts them.
  • The ICAAP runs two perspectives in parallel: the economic (present-value, all-material-risks, internal confidence level — home of the Extremfall) and the normative (multi-year regulatory-ratio projection, baseline + adverse Problemfall). A board-signed Capital Adequacy Statement owns the conclusion. ILAAP is the liquidity twin.
  • SREP scores four elements (business model, governance, capital, liquidity) and sets P2R (binding, sizes off capital risks, partly AT1/T2-eligible) and P2G (non-binding, bucketed off stress-test CET1 depletion). Stacking: OCR = 8% + P2R + CBR; P2G sits above the buffer; the MDA trigger is the combined buffer requirement, with the SyRB generally additive to O-SII/G-SII since CRD V.
  • Two separate NPE regimes: EBA/GL/2018/06 is process law (strategy, reduction, FBE) above a gross-NPL trigger; CRR Art. 47a–47c is the prudential backstop — three collateral tracks (unsecured to 100% by yr 3, movable-secured by yr 8, immovable-secured from yr 10), applying only to exposures created from 26 April 2019, whose shortfall is deducted from CET1. It is a clock, not a credit assessment.
  • Large exposures (CRR Part Four): post-CRM exposure to a single client or group of connected clients ≤ 25% of Tier 1; connected clients grouped by control OR economic interdependence (EBA/GL/2017/15, which gives only indicative reference points, not bright lines). The R-IPS earns a 0% risk weight (Art. 113(7)) and large-exposures exemption (Art. 400(2)) in exchange for a centralised risk-monitoring apparatus.
  • Outsourcing (EBA/GL/2019/02) demands a register, criticality assessment, exit strategy and retained oversight even for intra-sector shared services; DORA hardens ICT resilience into directly-applicable law; internal-governance and fit-and-proper rules close the loop.
🇦🇹 Zusammenfassung (Deutsch)
  • Die Aufsicht ist ein Vier-Schichten-Modell: CRR (Säule 1) → CRD-Puffer → Säule 2 (SREP setzt P2R verbindlich, P2G als Erwartung) → Wohlverhaltens-Leitlinien der EBA und DORA. EBA-Leitlinien gelten faktisch verbindlich, sobald die FMA sie übernimmt.
  • Der ICAAP fährt zwei Perspektiven parallel: die ökonomische (Barwertsicht, alle wesentlichen Risiken, interner Konfidenzgrad — der Extremfall) und die normative (mehrjährige Projektion der regulatorischen Quoten, Baseline + Problemfall). Das Capital Adequacy Statement der Geschäftsleitung trägt das Ergebnis. Der ILAAP ist das Liquiditäts-Pendant.
  • Der SREP bewertet vier Elemente (Geschäftsmodell, Governance, Kapital, Liquidität) und legt P2R (verbindlich) und P2G (Erwartung über dem Puffer, per Bucketing aus dem Stresstest-CET1-Abbau abgeleitet) fest. Reihenfolge: OCR = 8% + P2R + kombinierte Pufferanforderung; die Pufferanforderung ist der MDA-Auslöser, wobei der Systemrisikopuffer seit CRD V grundsätzlich additiv zum O-SII/G-SII-Puffer hinzukommt.
  • Zwei NPE-Regime: EBA/GL/2018/06 ist Prozessrecht (Strategie, Abbau, Stundung/FBE); CRR Art. 47a–47c ist der prudenzielle Backstop — drei Besicherungspfade (unbesichert 100% ab Jahr 3, beweglich besichert ab Jahr 8, immobilienbesichert ab Jahr 10), nur für ab 26. April 2019 begründete Forderungen, dessen Fehlbetrag direkt vom CET1 abgezogen wird. Der Backstop ist eine Uhr, keine Bonitätsprüfung.
  • Großkredite (CRR Teil 4): Forderung nach CRM an einen Kunden bzw. eine Gruppe verbundener Kunden ≤ 25% des Kernkapitals; Verbund über Kontrolle ODER wirtschaftliche Abhängigkeit (EBA/GL/2017/15, mit nur indikativen Richtwerten, keinen festen Schwellen). Das R-IPS erhält 0%-Risikogewicht (Art. 113(7)) und Großkredit-Ausnahme (Art. 400(2)) — als Gegenleistung für ein zentrales Risikoüberwachungssystem.
  • Auslagerung (EBA/GL/2019/02) verlangt Register, Kritikalitätsbewertung, Ausstiegsstrategie und behaltene Steuerung auch bei sektorinternen Dienstleistern; DORA verankert ICT-Resilienz als unmittelbar geltendes Recht.
