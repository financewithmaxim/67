Module 5 — Credit risk: deriving the IRB formula (CRR Art. 153/154) from Merton/Vasicek

Credit risk is the most material risk in the R-IPS, and the IRB risk-weight formula is the single most important equation you operate. This module builds it from the ground up — Merton's firm-value model → a one-factor world → the asymptotic (ASRF) portfolio limit → the exact CRR formula — then layers on the Leitfaden's own machinery: the 95% rescaling, the FX add-on with its PD-elasticity, and the HHI concentration surcharge.

By the end of this module you can…

  • Derive the conditional default probability from a Merton/Gaussian-copula single-factor model.
  • Explain why portfolio invariance (ASRF) is what makes a per-loan formula possible — and what it silently assumes.
  • Write the full CRR Art. 153 corporate formula and compute K, EL and UL by hand.
  • Show how the Problemfall swaps 99,9% → 95% with a single quantile substitution.
  • Apply the FX exposure add-on, the PD–FX elasticity (1,9), and the HHI granularity surcharge — and flag the Leitfaden's own numerical slip.

1 · Motivation — why a formula at all?

You have a portfolio of thousands of loans. You want one number: the capital that covers unexpected credit loss at 99,9% over a year. The honest way is a full Monte-Carlo simulation of correlated defaults. That is intractable to standardise, impossible to audit consistently across 400 Raiffeisenbanken, and opaque to a supervisor. Basel II's breakthrough was to find conditions under which the portfolio's 99,9% loss is just a sum of per-loan contributions — so each loan gets a risk weight that does not depend on the rest of the portfolio. That property is called portfolio invariance, and the model that delivers it is the Asymptotic Single-Risk-Factor (ASRF) model of Gordy (2003), built on Vasicek (2002), built on Merton (1974).

▶ The prize and its price

Portfolio invariance is what lets the Leitfaden compute credit risk loan-by-loan and simply add up. The price is two strong assumptions: (1) a single systematic factor drives all correlation, and (2) the portfolio is infinitely granular (no single name matters). Real portfolios violate (2) — which is exactly why the Leitfaden bolts on a concentration surcharge (§5). Understanding the assumption is understanding the fix.

2 · Concept — the Merton picture

Merton (1974): a firm defaults when the value of its assets \(A\) falls below its liabilities \(D\) at the horizon. Model the firm's standardised asset return \(V_i\) as a latent normal variable. The firm defaults when \(V_i\) drops below a threshold \(c_i\):

$$ \text{default}_i \iff V_i < c_i, \qquad V_i\sim N(0,1), \qquad PD_i=\Pr(V_i<c_i)=\Phi(c_i)\;\Rightarrow\; c_i=\Phi^{-1}(PD_i). $$
\(V_i\) = standardised asset value (the "creditworthiness index") of borrower \(i\); \(c_i\) = default threshold; \(\Phi\) = standard-normal CDF; \(PD_i\) = unconditional (through-the-cycle) probability of default. The threshold is just the PD's quantile.

Now make defaults correlated through a single common factor. Decompose each firm's asset return into a systematic part \(X\) (the economy) and an idiosyncratic part \(\varepsilon_i\) (the firm), with a loading \(\sqrt{\rho}\):

$$ V_i \;=\; \sqrt{\rho}\,X \;+\; \sqrt{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_i, \qquad X,\varepsilon_i \overset{iid}{\sim} N(0,1). $$
\(X\) = the single systematic risk factor (one number for the whole economy in a given year); \(\varepsilon_i\) = borrower-specific noise; \(\rho\) = the asset correlation — the share of a firm's variance driven by the common factor. By construction \(\operatorname{Var}(V_i)=\rho+(1-\rho)=1\), and \(\operatorname{Corr}(V_i,V_j)=\rho\). This is a Gaussian copula with one factor.

3 · Derivation — conditional PD, the ASRF limit, and the CRR formula

3.1 The conditional default probability

Condition on a realised state of the economy \(X=x\). Given \(x\), the borrowers default independently (their only link was \(X\)). Borrower \(i\) defaults iff \(V_i<\Phi^{-1}(PD_i)\), i.e.

$$ \sqrt{\rho}\,x + \sqrt{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_i < \Phi^{-1}(PD) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \varepsilon_i < \frac{\Phi^{-1}(PD)-\sqrt{\rho}\,x}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}. $$ Taking the probability over \(\varepsilon_i\sim N(0,1)\): $$ \boxed{\,p(x)\;=\;\Pr(\text{default}\mid X=x)\;=\;\Phi\!\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(PD)-\sqrt{\rho}\,x}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right).} $$
\(p(x)\) = the default rate in economic state \(x\). A bad economy is a low \(x\) (assets fall). As \(x\downarrow\), the numerator rises, so \(p(x)\uparrow\): defaults spike together in downturns. That co-movement is the whole point.

3.2 The ASRF limit — why this becomes a per-loan formula

In an infinitely granular portfolio (many small exposures), the law of large numbers makes the realised portfolio loss rate converge to its conditional expectation \(p(x)\): idiosyncratic risk diversifies away, and only the systematic factor \(X\) remains. So the portfolio loss is a deterministic, monotone function of the single factor \(X\). The 99,9%-worst portfolio loss therefore happens at the 99,9%-worst value of \(X\).

The worst \(X\) at confidence \(q\) is its lower tail (bad economy): \(x_q=\Phi^{-1}(1-q)=-\Phi^{-1}(q)\). Substitute \(x=-\Phi^{-1}(q)\) into \(p(x)\):

$$ \boxed{\,PD_{\text{cond}}(q)\;=\;\Phi\!\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(PD)+\sqrt{\rho}\,\Phi^{-1}(q)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right).} $$
This is the stressed / conditional PD: the default rate in a 1-in-\(1/(1-q)\) economy. Note the sign has flipped to + because a bad economy is the lower tail of \(X\). For the CRR, \(q=99{,}9\%\Rightarrow\Phi^{-1}(q)=3{,}0902\). For the Leitfaden Problemfall, \(q=95\%\Rightarrow\Phi^{-1}(q)=1{,}6449\) — the only thing that changes is this quantile.
The capital formula is "stressed EL minus expected EL"

Expected loss uses the average PD; the conditional loss uses the stressed PD. Capital covers the difference — the unexpected part:

$$ K \;=\; LGD\cdot PD_{\text{cond}}(q)\;-\;LGD\cdot PD \;=\; LGD\cdot\Big[\Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{\Phi^{-1}(PD)+\sqrt{\rho}\,\Phi^{-1}(q)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\Big)-PD\Big]. $$

Subtracting \(LGD\cdot PD\) (the EL) is the formula-level version of the Leitfaden's accounting rule: capital carries UL only; EL lives in the Deckungsmasse as excess/shortfall.

3.3 The full CRR Art. 153 corporate formula

The CRR plugs three regulatory choices into this skeleton: a PD-dependent asset correlation \(R\), a maturity adjustment, and a fixed mapping to risk-weighted assets.

$$ R(PD)=0{,}12\,\frac{1-e^{-50\,PD}}{1-e^{-50}}+0{,}24\left(1-\frac{1-e^{-50\,PD}}{1-e^{-50}}\right) $$
Asset correlation interpolates between 0,24 (low PD) and 0,12 (high PD) as PD rises. Intuition: high-PD names default for idiosyncratic reasons, so they are less tied to the systematic factor — lower correlation. regulatory values.
$$ b(PD)=\big(0{,}11852-0{,}05478\,\ln PD\big)^2, \qquad \text{Maturity adj.}=\frac{1+(M-2{,}5)\,b(PD)}{1-1{,}5\,b(PD)} $$
\(b(PD)\) = maturity-adjustment smoothing; \(M\) = effective maturity. The factor is normalised to 1 at \(M=2{,}5\) years — which is precisely why the Leitfaden's fixed \(M=2{,}5\) makes the adjustment vanish (see §4).
$$ K=\Bigg[LGD\cdot\Phi\!\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(PD)+\sqrt{R}\,\Phi^{-1}(q)}{\sqrt{1-R}}\right)-LGD\cdot PD\Bigg]\cdot\frac{1+(M-2{,}5)\,b}{1-1{,}5\,b} $$ $$ RWA = K\cdot 12{,}5\cdot EAD, \qquad \text{UL (capital)} = K\cdot EAD. $$
\(K\) = capital requirement per unit EAD; \(EAD\) = exposure at default; the \(12{,}5=1/0{,}08\) converts capital to RWA. Retail (Art. 154) uses the same conditional-PD core but with fixed correlations (no \(R(PD)\) curve: 0,15 mortgages; 0,04 QRRE; a 0,03–0,16 curve for other retail) and no maturity adjustment.
🟣 What the Leitfaden fixes vs what the CRR forces

regulatory The functional form, \(R(PD)\), \(b(PD)\), the 99,9% calibration, and the 12,5 factor are CRR Art. 153/154 — not negotiable.   ÖRS choice The Leitfaden then fixes inputs for tractability: M = 2,5y (so the maturity adjustment = 1), unsecured LGD = 45% for FI/Sov/LRG/CIU (and pooled 45,5/54,4/55,8% for Corporate/Retail — Module 15), PDs from the master scale, and — crucially — it swaps the 99,9% quantile for 95% in the Problemfall. Each is a proportionality decision trading precision for comparability across hundreds of banks.

4 · Worked example — UL by hand, both scenarios

Worked example — a single corporate exposure

Inputs: \(EAD=\)€1.000.000, rating 2,5 ⇒ \(PD=1{,}3669\%\) (master scale midpoint), \(LGD=45{,}5\%\) (Corporate pool), \(M=2{,}5\) (so maturity adj. = 1).

Step 1 — asset correlation. With \(PD=0{,}013669\): \(\;\frac{1-e^{-50\cdot0{,}013669}}{1-e^{-50}}=1-e^{-0{,}6835}=0{,}4951.\)

$$ R = 0{,}12\cdot0{,}4951 + 0{,}24\cdot(1-0{,}4951) = 0{,}0594 + 0{,}1212 = 0{,}1806. $$

Step 2 — conditional PD (Extremfall, q = 99,9%, \(\Phi^{-1}(q)=3{,}0902\)). With \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}013669)=-2{,}2064\), \(\sqrt{R}=0{,}4250\), \(\sqrt{1-R}=0{,}9046\):

$$ PD_{\text{cond}}=\Phi\!\left(\frac{-2{,}2064+0{,}4250\cdot3{,}0902}{0{,}9046}\right)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{-0{,}8930}{0{,}9046}\right)=\Phi(-0{,}9872)=16{,}18\%. $$

Step 3 — capital per unit & UL. \(K = 0{,}455\cdot(0{,}1618-0{,}013669)\cdot1 = 0{,}455\cdot0{,}1481 = 0{,}06740.\)
UL (Extremfall) = K·EAD = €67.400.   EL = \(LGD\cdot PD\cdot EAD = 0{,}455\cdot0{,}013669\cdot10^6 =\) €6.220 (sits in the Deckungsmasse as excess/shortfall).

Now the Problemfall — change only the quantile to \(q=95\%,\ \Phi^{-1}(q)=1{,}6449\):

$$ PD_{\text{cond}}=\Phi\!\left(\frac{-2{,}2064+0{,}4250\cdot1{,}6449}{0{,}9046}\right)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{-1{,}5074}{0{,}9046}\right)=\Phi(-1{,}6664)=4{,}78\%. $$

\(K = 0{,}455\cdot(0{,}0478-0{,}013669)=0{,}455\cdot0{,}0341=0{,}01552.\)   UL (Problemfall) = €15.520.

The Problemfall UL is roughly a quarter of the Extremfall UL — entirely from substituting one quantile. This is VaR vertical consistency (Module 4) operating inside the credit formula.

5 · In practice — the Leitfaden's credit machinery

5.1 Exposure and Risikoposition

The Risikoposition = Exposure − interne Sicherheiten per customer, floored at zero. Exposure (§5.1) sums: the balance (book or market value for securities), weighted off-balance items (Haftungswert incl. letters of credit, derivatives, repos), weighted undrawn committed lines, and weighted (≥20%) undrawn internal lines. Netting and CSA cash collateral reduce the Saldo; consortium shares are deducted; provisions are not deducted from the Saldo (they feed the excess/shortfall instead). The ≥20% on internal lines is an ÖRS prudence choice — legally internal lines are no exposure, but the guide reserves part anyway.

5.2 FX credit-risk add-on and the PD–FX elasticity

An FX loan's euro exposure rises when the foreign currency appreciates, so the Leitfaden scales the exposure up by the scenario-scaled annual FX volatility (3-year history) and treats the uplift as unsecured (collateral value unchanged) — which raises the blended LGD. FI and Sovereigns get no FX credit risk (materiality / already captured on consolidation).

Separately, a weaker local currency raises the borrower's default probability. The guide estimates this with a regression of PD on GDP and FX, giving an FX-elasticity of the point-in-time PD of 1,9 (190%) ÖRS choice:

$$ PD_{\text{PiT}} = PD_{\text{TtC}}\cdot\big(1 + 1{,}9\cdot \Delta_{\text{fx}}\big) $$
\(\Delta_{\text{fx}}\) = relative depreciation of the local currency. Example with the governing parameter 1,9: a \(PD_{\text{TtC}}=2\%\) and a 25% depreciation give \(PD_{\text{PiT}}=2\%\cdot(1+1{,}9\cdot0{,}25)=2{,}95\%.\)
⚠ Examiner trap — the Leitfaden contradicts itself here

The guide's own worked example reads "\(2\% + 273\%\cdot25\%\cdot2\% = 3{,}005\%\)". That uses 273%, not the stated elasticity of 1,9 (190%) — and even 273% gives 3,365%, while the printed result 3,005% implies an elasticity of ~2,0. The three figures are mutually inconsistent: it is a documentation slip left over from an earlier parameterisation (the version history confirms the FX elasticity was updated). For exam and production purposes, 1,9 is the governing parameter; flag the example as an erratum. Being able to spot this is exactly the "defend the methodology" skill the course targets.

5.3 Concentration — the HHI granularity surcharge

The ASRF formula assumed infinite granularity. Real corporate books are lumpy, so the Leitfaden adds a Granularitätsaufschlag = the gap between the UL of the real portfolio and an infinitely granular one of the same character, approximated via a Herfindahl–Hirschman index:

$$ HHI = hhi_{21} + hhi_{22}, \qquad hhi_{21}=\sum_i s_i^2, \qquad hhi_{22}=s_m\,(1-S_m), $$ $$ s_i=\frac{\text{open single position}_i}{\text{open total corporate position}}, \qquad S_m=\sum_{i} s_i. $$
\(s_i\) = share of open risk position \(i\) (Exposure − interne Sicherheiten − Vorsorgen) among the top Corporates (max 110 positions, per GvK); \(hhi_{22}\) is a tail correction for the positions beyond the top set (\(s_m\) = the \(m\)-th share, \(S_m\) = cumulative share). \(HHI\in[1/n,1]\): 1 = a single borrower. Surcharge = UL · HHI; the new UL = \(UL\,(1+HHI)\).
Mini-example

If the corporate UL is €500.000 and the computed \(HHI=0{,}04\), the granularity surcharge is \(0{,}04\cdot500.000=\)€20.000, giving a concentration-adjusted corporate UL of €520.000. A perfectly diversified book (\(HHI\to1/n\approx0\)) adds almost nothing; a book dominated by a few names adds a lot.

5.4 The other fixed inputs

  • PD by Ratingstufe from the master scale (geometric-mean midpoints): 0,5→0,0533% … 2,5→1,3669% … 4,5→35,03% … 5,x→100%; NR→35,03%. FI/Sov/LRG/CIU use RBI-group PDs.
  • LGD: 45% unsecured (regulatory F-IRB); pooled 45,5% Corporate, 54,4% Retail selbständig, 55,8% Retail unselbständig (Module 15); covered bonds 12,5%; defaults 80% (5,1) / 100% (5,2).
  • M = 2,5y for IRB-formula credits (maturity adj. = 1); M = 1y for "nur Wertpapiere" (their downgrade risk sits in credit-spread/market risk — avoids double counting).
  • Migration risk assessed via rating-model migration matrices (MMATRIX, annual).

6 · Nuances & traps

⚠ Bad economy is the LOWER tail of X — mind the sign

In \(p(x)\) the systematic term is \(-\sqrt\rho\,x\); a downturn is a small/negative \(x\). When you substitute the worst case you set \(x=-\Phi^{-1}(q)\), which flips the sign to \(+\sqrt\rho\,\Phi^{-1}(q)\) in the conditional-PD formula. Getting this sign wrong gives you the 0,1% best case instead of the worst — a classic derivation error.

⚠ The formula already subtracts EL — don't subtract it twice

\(K\) is built as stressed EL − EL. It is pure UL. If you then also net EL out of the capital number you double-count. EL is removed once, and it reappears (with the opposite sign) as the excess/shortfall in the Deckungsmasse.

⚠ Single factor = one source of correlation

The ASRF model has exactly one systematic factor, so it cannot represent sector or regional concentration — every borrower correlates with every other only through the one economy variable. That is precisely why concentration risk must be added outside the formula (the HHI surcharge), and why country/sector limits live in separate modules. If an examiner asks "what does the IRB formula miss?", the answer is name, sector and regional concentration — and migration beyond the through-the-cycle PD.

Recap

  • Merton: default when latent asset value \(V_i<\Phi^{-1}(PD)\); one factor: \(V_i=\sqrt\rho X+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_i\).
  • Conditional PD \(=\Phi\big(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(PD)+\sqrt\rho\,\Phi^{-1}(q)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\big)\); ASRF + granularity ⇒ portfolio loss is a function of \(X\) alone ⇒ a per-loan formula.
  • \(K=[LGD\cdot PD_{\text{cond}}-LGD\cdot PD]\cdot\)(maturity adj.); CRR Art. 153 (corporate, \(R(PD)\), maturity adj.) vs Art. 154 (retail, fixed \(R\), none).
  • Problemfall = swap \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}999)=3{,}0902\) for \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}95)=1{,}6449\) — nothing else.
  • Leitfaden add-ons: FX exposure uplift + PD-elasticity 1,9 (mind the printed-example erratum), and the HHI granularity surcharge for concentration; M = 2,5y fixes the maturity adjustment to 1.
