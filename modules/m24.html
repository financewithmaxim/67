Module 24 — Recovery, resolution, MREL/BRRD & deep deposit guarantee (DGSD/ESAEG)

Going-concern capital (Modules 13–18) answers one question: can the bank absorb losses and keep trading? This module answers the next two — what happens when it cannot, and who pays the insured depositor in the meantime. We build the full Krisenmanagement (crisis-management) stack — recovery planning, early intervention, resolution, MREL and deposit guarantee — and then show why, for a typical Raiffeisen primary bank, the operative answer is none of the glamorous resolution tools but ordinary Insolvenz (insolvency) backstopped by a single sector deposit guarantee scheme, with the Austrian transposition (BaSAG and ESAEG) as the operative national law.

By the end of this module you can…

  • Distinguish Sanierung (recovery, a going-concern duty of the bank) from frühzeitiges Eingreifen (early intervention, a supervisory power) and from Abwicklung (resolution, a gone-concern power of the authority), and place all three relative to ordinary insolvency.
  • Name the Austrian transposition statutes — BaSAG for BRRD/BRRD2 and ESAEG for the DGSD — and locate the FMA's recovery, early-intervention and resolution powers in them.
  • Reconstruct the public-interest assessment (PIA) and explain why most Raiffeisen primary banks carry a liquidation strategy rather than a resolution strategy.
  • Derive the MREL calibration as loss-absorption + recapitalisation, compute it for a worked balance sheet, and run the M-MDA check as a CET1-specific test that does not double-count CET1.
  • State the core MREL eligibility conditions (CRR Art. 72b / BRRD Art. 45b), including the retail-sale restriction that bites in Raiffeisen distribution.
  • Reproduce the DGSD/ESAEG mechanics and the DGS's dual role — payout in liquidation and a capped contribution in resolution (BRRD Art. 109) — and defend which safety mechanism bites first in the sector.

1 · Motivation — why this exists

Capital buffers (Module 13) are a going-concern (laufender Betrieb) device: they let a bank absorb losses while still meeting its authorisation conditions and serving customers. But a buffer is finite. Once it is exhausted — or once an idiosyncratic shock makes the bank unable to pay its obligations as they fall due — the question is no longer "how much loss can it absorb?" but "how do we wind this down without a disorderly run, a fire-sale, and a queue of depositors at the branch door?"

Before 2014 the honest answer in most of Europe was: either a taxpayer-funded bailout, or a messy insolvency that froze deposits for months. The Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD, Directive 2014/59/EU, recast and amended by BRRD2, Directive 2019/879) and the Single Resolution Mechanism (SRM, Regulation 806/2014) were the post-crisis answer. In Austria both are transposed by the Bundesgesetz über die Sanierung und Abwicklung von Banken (BaSAG — the Sanierungs- und Abwicklungsgesetz), which is the legal basis for the FMA's recovery, early-intervention and resolution powers and the statute a sector controller actually cites. Their organising principle is a single sentence worth memorising for the Aufsichtsrat: shareholders and creditors, not taxpayers, bear the cost of failure — and the machinery exists so that this can happen over a weekend rather than over a decade.

▶ The layers, in one breath

Recovery (Sanierung) is what the bank does to save itself while still alive. Early intervention (frühzeitiges Eingreifen) is what the supervisor may impose while the bank is still a going concern but deteriorating. Resolution (Abwicklung) is what the resolution authority does to wind it down in an orderly way once it is failing and saving it serves a public interest. Ordinary insolvency (Regelinsolvenz) plus the deposit guarantee scheme (Einlagensicherung) is what happens to everyone else — which, in the Raiffeisen sector, means almost everyone.

This module sits downstream of the buffer cascade (Module 13), the MDA logic (Module 14), the ICAAP/ILAAP and SREP machinery (Modules 19–21), and the liquidity framework (Modules 22–23). It is the last line: what the cascade is protecting against, and the safety net underneath it.

2 · Concept & logic

2.1 Recovery planning — BRRD Art. 5–9 / BaSAG

A Sanierungsplan (recovery plan) is a living document the bank itself writes and maintains under BRRD Art. 5 (transposed in BaSAG). It is emphatically not a resolution plan: it assumes the bank survives. Its skeleton is fixed:

  • Recovery indicators (Sanierungsindikatoren) — a calibrated set of capital, liquidity, profitability and market-based triggers (EBA guidelines EBA/GL/2015/02). Each indicator has a threshold that, when breached, forces management to consider — and the board to decide on — escalation. The classic four families: CET1 ratio, LCR/NSFR, return-on-assets, and a market/macro indicator (e.g. CDS spread or rating action proxy for unlisted banks).
  • Menu of recovery options (Handlungsoptionen) — a pre-analysed list: capital measures (rights issue, AT1 issuance, retained-earnings retention), liquidity measures (asset sales, central-bank-eligible collateral mobilisation, deposit campaigns), and business measures (disposal of portfolios or subsidiaries, cost cuts). Each option carries an estimated capital/liquidity impact, a time-to-effect, and an impediment analysis.
  • Scenarios — at least one idiosyncratic, one system-wide, and one combined stress, each severe enough to threaten viability, with the options tested against them.
  • Governance — who declares escalation, on what timeline, and how the indicators map to the bank's normal risk appetite framework.
▶ Group plans, IPS plans, and simplified obligations

BRRD Art. 7 lets a group submit a group recovery plan instead of solo plans. Art. 4 permits simplified obligations for institutions whose failure is unlikely to have a significant negative effect on financial markets — assessed on size, interconnectedness, substitutability and complexity. Crucially for the sector: where an institutional protection scheme (IPS, Module 12) exists, the EBA guidelines and national practice allow recovery planning to be partly performed at IPS level, because the IPS itself is a recovery mechanism — it can inject capital and liquidity into a member before that member ever reaches its own recovery triggers. The R-IPS (the Raiffeisen IPS) is thus the first, not the last, line of defence.

2.2 Early intervention — BRRD Art. 27–29

Between a recovery-indicator breach and the declaration that a bank is failing or likely to fail there is a deliberate intermediate rung: early intervention measures (frühzeitiges Eingreifen) under BRRD Art. 27–29 (transposed in BaSAG). These are supervisory powers — exercised by the FMA / the SSM — and they kick in when an institution infringes, or is likely in the near future to infringe, its prudential requirements (the trigger is typically expressed relative to the own-funds requirement plus a margin), but is not yet gone.

  • Art. 27 — the supervisor may require management to implement the recovery plan, draw up an action programme with a timetable, convene the shareholders' meeting, require a debt-restructuring plan, demand changes to the business strategy or to legal/operational structures, and obtain the information needed to update the resolution plan.
  • Art. 28 — power to remove and replace senior management or the management body where early intervention under Art. 27 is insufficient.
  • Art. 29 — appointment of a temporary administrator (vorläufiger Verwalter), either to work alongside or to replace the management body for a limited period.
▶ Why the controller cares about the middle rung

The escalation ladder is recovery-indicator breach → management escalation under the bank's own plan → supervisory early intervention (Art. 27–29) → FOLTF determination → resolution or liquidation. Skipping the early-intervention rung in a board narrative makes failure look like a cliff. It is not: there is a supervised, going-concern corridor in which the FMA can force the recovery plan to be executed, change the management, or install a vorläufiger Verwalter before anyone reaches FOLTF. For a sector bank this corridor usually runs in parallel with — and is pre-empted by — R-IPS intervention (§6.1).

2.3 Resolution — the SRM, the SRB and the toolbox

If a bank is failing or likely to fail (FOLTF — "failing or likely to fail", BRRD Art. 32), supervisory and recovery options including early intervention are exhausted, and resolution is in the public interest, the resolution authority steps in. In the Banking Union the architecture is two-tier: the Single Resolution Board (SRB) in Brussels is directly responsible for significant institutions and cross-border groups; national resolution authorities handle the rest. In Austria the NRA is the Finanzmarktaufsicht (FMA), acting under BaSAG and executing through the SRM where applicable, funded by the Single Resolution Fund (SRF).

BRRD Art. 37 gives the authority four resolution tools (Abwicklungsinstrumente):

ToolBRRD Art.MechanismTypical use
Bail-in (Gläubigerbeteiligung)43–55Write down / convert shareholders' and creditors' claims into equity to recapitalise the entity in place.Open-bank resolution of a systemic institution; the reason MREL exists.
Sale of business (Unternehmensveräußerung)38–39Sell all or part to a private purchaser, no shareholder consent needed.A clean franchise with a willing buyer.
Bridge institution (Brückeninstitut)40–41Transfer good assets/liabilities to a temporary publicly controlled entity; sell later.No immediate buyer; preserve critical functions.
Asset separation (Ausgliederung von Vermögenswerten)42Move bad assets to an asset-management vehicle ("bad bank"); only alongside another tool.Clean up a balance sheet to make the rest saleable.

All four sit behind two guardrails. First, the NCWO principle — no creditor worse off (kein Gläubiger schlechter gestellt, BRRD Art. 34(1)(g) and 73–75): no creditor may end up worse than they would have been in ordinary insolvency, with an independent valuation and a compensation right if they are. Second, the bail-in before public funds rule: shareholders and creditors must absorb losses equal to at least 8% of total liabilities and own funds before the SRF can contribute (BRRD Art. 44(5); Art. 37(10)).

2.4 The public-interest assessment — and why most Raiffeisen banks liquidate

The hinge of the whole framework is the public-interest assessment (PIA, öffentliches Interesse) under BRRD Art. 32(5)/SRMR Art. 18(5). Resolution is used only if winding the bank up under normal insolvency proceedings would not meet the resolution objectives — continuity of critical functions, avoiding contagion, protecting public funds, protecting covered depositors and client assets — to the same extent. If ordinary insolvency does the job, resolution is not in the public interest, and the bank gets a liquidation strategy (Liquidationsstrategie).

▶ The sector reality

A single Raiffeisenbank in, say, Niederösterreich provides no unique critical function: its lending and payment services are substitutable by neighbouring sector banks; its covered deposits are protected by the DGS; its failure, isolated, does not threaten the system. The PIA therefore typically concludes negative — no public interest in resolution — and the planned strategy is liquidation under ordinary insolvency with DGS payout. The PIA outcome itself is a regulatory value driven by Art. 32(5); how the sector organises its mutual support around that outcome is the ÖRS modelling decision. The trade-off named explicitly: a liquidation strategy means much lower (often zero) external MREL for the primary bank — but it also means no orderly going-concern rescue by the State, so the burden of preventing failure shifts onto the R-IPS and the DGS. Cheaper in capital, heavier in mutual solidarity. Note that "no public interest" is not a function of size below an SRB significance threshold: a large bank can still fail the PIA if its functions are substitutable, and a small one can pass it if it is uniquely critical. The PIA, not the balance-sheet total, decides.

3 · Derivation — MREL calibration

The bail-in tool only works if there is something to bail in. A bank funded entirely by insured deposits and secured liabilities has no loss-absorbing layer that can be written down without triggering NCWO problems or hitting protected depositors. MREL — the minimum requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities (Mindestanforderung an Eigenmittel und berücksichtigungsfähige Verbindlichkeiten) — forces the bank to pre-position a stack of bail-inable liabilities. (For global systemically important institutions, G-SIIs, the internationally-agreed analogue is TLAC, total loss-absorbing capacity, hard-wired into CRR Art. 92a–92b; MREL is the broader EU requirement applying to all banks.)

3.1 The two-component logic

The calibration answers two questions in sequence. First, how much loss must be absorbed to bring the bank to the point of failure? Second, how much capital must be rebuilt so the resolved entity is viable and credible again? Hence:

$$ \text{MREL} = \underbrace{\text{LAA}}_{\text{loss absorption}} + \underbrace{\text{RCA}}_{\text{recapitalisation}} $$
where LAA = loss-absorption amount, the capital the bank must hold today; RCA = recapitalisation amount, the capital needed to restore viability after resolution.

BRRD2 Art. 45c expresses both legs on two denominators simultaneously — risk-weighted (TREA) and leverage (TEM, total exposure measure) — and the binding requirement is the higher in money terms. Take the risk-weighted leg. Let:

$$ \text{LAA}_{\text{RWA}} = P_1 + P_2\text{R} \qquad \text{RCA}_{\text{RWA}} = P_1 + P_2\text{R} + \text{MCC} $$
where \(P_1\) = Pillar 1 own-funds requirement (8% of TREA, Module 13); \(P_2\text{R}\) = the bank-specific Pillar 2 requirement from SREP (Module 19); MCC = the market confidence charge (Marktvertrauenspuffer), the SRB's add-on so the post-resolution bank can re-enter funding markets. Under the SRB's published default the MCC is set at the combined buffer requirement minus 125 basis points, floored at zero and subject to case-by-case adjustment — not simply CBR minus the entire countercyclical buffer.

Summing (using the SRB default MCC):

$$ \text{MREL}_{\text{RWA}} = \underbrace{(P_1 + P_2\text{R})}_{\text{LAA}} + \underbrace{(P_1 + P_2\text{R} + \text{MCC})}_{\text{RCA}} = 2\,(P_1 + P_2\text{R}) + \text{MCC} $$

The structure is intuitive: you need roughly twice your own-funds requirement (once to absorb the losses that killed you, once to rebuild), plus a confidence cushion. The combined buffer requirement (CBR) is then layered on top of MREL when expressed as a usable ratio — and that layering is exactly what creates the M-MDA constraint in §3.4. Note carefully: the buffer sits on top, it is not folded into the MREL stack — confusing the two is the double-counting error §4 is built to prevent.

3.2 The leverage leg

The same loss-absorption-plus-recapitalisation logic is expressed on the leverage denominator (TEM). Here the recapitalisation amount is calibrated to restore the leverage ratio requirement (LRR) — the baseline 3% Pillar 1 leverage requirement (Module 13), plus any leverage-ratio P2R or G-SII leverage buffer that applies. The leverage LAA+RCA does not mechanically equal twice the LRR; it is the LAA needed to reach the point of failure plus whatever RCA restores the binding leverage requirement, and the MCC is generally not added on the leverage leg. The binding MREL is then \(\max\) of the two legs in money terms. In a deposit-funded sector bank with a low risk-weight book the leverage leg can in principle bind, so the controller must always compute both and read off the higher — never assume the risk-weighted leg wins.

3.3 Subordination and MREL eligibility

Bail-in is credible only if the liabilities you intend to write down actually can be written down without breaching NCWO against pari passu senior creditors who were excluded. Hence MREL has a subordination (Nachrangigkeit) sub-requirement: a portion must be met with own funds and subordinated eligible liabilities ranking below ordinary senior debt. For resolution entities the floor is the higher of (i) 8% of total liabilities and own funds (TLOF) and (ii) a "prudential formula" tied to \(P_1+P_2\text{R}\) and buffers — capped per BRRD2 Art. 45b. Both the precise prudential-formula calibration and the cap are live and contestable: confirm the current Art. 45b calibration and cap. The subordination requirement also has a leverage (TEM) expression, not only a TLOF/TREA one — the binding subordination floor is the higher of the risk-based and leverage-based formulations. The 8%-of-TLOF figure is the same threshold that unlocks the SRF: it is no coincidence that the minimum bail-in-before-fund and the minimum subordination are aligned.

▶ MREL eligibility — the conditions, not just "more capital"

An instrument counts toward MREL only if it satisfies the eligibility conditions of CRR Art. 72b / BRRD Art. 45b. The core gates: the instrument is issued and fully paid up; it is not funded directly or indirectly by the institution itself; the claim is not subject to set-off or netting that would undermine loss absorption; it has a residual maturity of at least one year; it is not redeemable, callable or repurchased before maturity without prior authority consent; and it is not subject to acceleration rights outside resolution. For subordinated eligible liabilities sold to retail, BRRD adds a distribution safeguard: institutions may sell them to a retail client only where that client's portfolio meets a size/suitability test — in practice a minimum initial investment of EUR 10,000 or a portfolio threshold around EUR 500,000 (verify the current BRRD Art. 44a transposition in BaSAG). This retail-sale restriction matters specifically in the Raiffeisen distribution model, where bail-inable instruments could otherwise be placed across a broad cooperative member base. regulatory value

▶ Liquidation entities: MREL ≈ own funds (post-2024)

For a bank whose resolution strategy is liquidation, there is nothing to recapitalise — RCA = 0. Historically the SRB still set MREL equal to the LAA (i.e. ≈ own-funds requirement). Directive 2024/1174 and the SRB's 2024 MREL policy changed this: no MREL above the LAA is set for liquidation entities unless specifically justified to protect financial stability, mitigate contagion, or fund deposit protection. Concretely: a typical Raiffeisen primary bank with a liquidation strategy carries no external MREL beyond its ordinary own-funds requirement. regulatory value The trade-off: this is proportionality in its purest form — no costly subordinated debt issuance for a small bank — paid for by the sector's collective willingness to absorb failures through the IPS and DGS rather than through market-funded bail-in.

3.4 The buffer interaction — M-MDA

Recall the MDA logic (Module 14): if a bank dips into its combined buffer requirement on the own-funds side, distributions are restricted. BRRD2 created a parallel constraint on the MREL side. The key mechanic is CET1-specific: CET1 used to meet MREL cannot simultaneously be counted towards the CBR. So you must first allocate CET1 to the MREL requirement (after exhausting any non-CET1 eligible instruments against MREL), and only the residual CET1 is available to satisfy the CBR stacked above. If that residual is short of the CBR, the bank breaches the M-MDA (the maximum distributable amount related to MREL, BRRD2 Art. 16a). The authority may restrict distributions, AT1 coupons and variable remuneration — generally after a nine-month grace window, after which restriction becomes mandatory. The mental model: CET1 is a scarce resource spent first on MREL; the buffer sits on top and must be covered by what is left — the same CET1 euro cannot be spent twice.

4 · Worked example — MREL and the buffer stack

▶ A mid-sized regional bank with a resolution strategy

Suppose — contrary to the sector norm, to make the arithmetic bite — a regional bank is assessed as a resolution entity. Inputs (all illustrative):

  • TREA (risk-weighted exposure amount) = EUR 4,000m
  • \(P_1\) = 8.0% of TREA ·   \(P_2\text{R}\) = 2.0% of TREA
  • Combined buffer requirement (CBR) = 3.5% of TREA (2.5% conservation + 1.0% O-SII/systemic, CCyB = 0)
  • Market confidence charge MCC = CBR − 125 bps = 3.5% − 1.25% = 2.25% of TREA (SRB default, floored at zero)
  • Total exposure measure (TEM, leverage denominator) = EUR 9,000m (illustrative)

Step 1 — risk-weighted MREL. Apply §3.1 with the SRB-default MCC:

$$ \text{MREL}_{\text{RWA}} = 2(P_1 + P_2\text{R}) + \text{MCC} = 2(8.0\% + 2.0\%) + 2.25\% = 22.25\%\ \text{of TREA} $$

In money: \(22.25\% \times 4{,}000 = \textbf{EUR 890m}\). (If you instead used the cruder CBR − CCyB = 3.5% for the MCC, you would get 23.5% = EUR 940m — which is precisely the overstatement the MCC-rule correction in §3.1 warns against.)

Step 2 — leverage-based MREL. The leverage leg is calibrated off the leverage ratio requirement (§3.2), not by a mechanical doubling. With LRR = 3.0% of TEM and an illustrative RCA restoring that requirement, suppose the calibrated leverage MREL works out to 6.0% of TEM — a figure used here purely to make the comparison concrete, not a derived rule:

$$ \text{MREL}_{\text{LR}} = 6.0\% \times 9{,}000 = \textbf{EUR 540m}\ \text{(illustrative)} $$

Step 3 — binding requirement. Take the higher in euros: \(\max(890,\ 540) = \textbf{EUR 890m}\), i.e. on these illustrative numbers the risk-weighted leg binds. Because the leverage calibration is assumption-driven, the controller must recompute both legs from the actual SRB-set figures before concluding which leg binds — do not treat the risk-weighted leg as automatically binding.

Step 4 — the M-MDA check, done correctly (CET1-specific). The CBR must be met with CET1 on top of MREL, and CET1 used inside MREL cannot also count toward the CBR. Suppose the bank holds EUR 1,000m of eligible instruments: EUR 700m CET1 and EUR 300m subordinated (non-CET1) eligible debt. Walk the CET1 allocation explicitly:

$$ \text{CET1 consumed by MREL} = \text{MREL} - \text{non-CET1 eligible} = 940 - 300 = \textbf{EUR 640m} $$
where we use the headline MREL of EUR 940m the SRB actually set here (the bank's binding requirement in this scenario); EUR 300m of subordinated debt is applied first, leaving EUR 640m of MREL that must be met with CET1.
$$ \text{Residual CET1 above MREL} = \text{CET1 held} - \text{CET1 consumed} = 700 - 640 = \textbf{EUR 60m} $$

The CBR requires CET1 of \(3.5\% \times 4{,}000 = \textbf{EUR 140m}\). Only EUR 60m of CET1 is left after MREL is met. The shortfall is therefore:

$$ \text{CBR requirement} - \text{residual CET1} = 140 - 60 = \textbf{EUR 80m} $$

The EUR 80m shortfall sits in the buffer region → M-MDA bites: distributions, AT1 coupons and variable remuneration can be capped. Note that this is the only correct route to the number. The tempting wrong route — comparing total eligible instruments (EUR 1,000m) against a "stacked requirement" of MREL + CBR = (23.5% + 3.5%) × 4,000 = EUR 1,080m and reading off 1,080 − 1,000 = EUR 80m — gives the same answer here by coincidence, but it is methodologically wrong: it lets the EUR 300m of non-CET1 subordinated debt implicitly count toward the CBR, which is exactly the double-counting M-MDA forbids. The lesson for the controller: hitting the headline MREL ratio is necessary but not sufficient; the CBR-on-top test, run on CET1 only, is the one that actually gates the dividend.

⚠ Don't double-count the CET1

The single most common modelling error is counting one CET1 euro towards both MREL and the CBR — or, equivalently, comparing total eligible instruments against a MREL + CBR stacked figure, which silently credits non-CET1 subordinated debt against the CBR. The whole point of M-MDA is that the CBR can only be met with CET1 that is not already spent on MREL. When you build the sector's MREL monitoring in R/SQL, model CET1 as a scarce resource: subordinated/eligible non-CET1 debt is applied to MREL first, then CET1 fills the rest of MREL, and only the residual CET1 is tested against the CBR — never as two independent ratios checked in parallel.

5 · In practice — the sector

5.1 What a Raiffeisen primary bank actually carries

Stack the pieces. The PIA for a single primary bank is almost always negative → liquidation strategy → under the 2024 policy, no external MREL beyond own funds. There is therefore typically no subordinated-debt programme, no bail-in stack to maintain, and no M-MDA monitoring at the primary level. The going-concern capital cascade (Modules 13–14) is the relevant constraint; the gone-concern machinery is dormant for that entity. The exception is the top of the sector: the central institution / the consolidated group may itself be a significant institution under SRB remit with a genuine resolution strategy and a real MREL — and there the §4 arithmetic is live, including the retail-sale restriction on any subordinated eligible liabilities placed through the cooperative base.

5.2 Deposit guarantee, deep — DGSD 2014/49 and the ESAEG

If a primary bank fails and is liquidated, the depositor is made whole not by resolution but by the Einlagensicherung. The EU frame is the Deposit Guarantee Schemes Directive (DGSD, Directive 2014/49/EU); the Austrian transposition is the Einlagensicherungs- und Anlegerentschädigungsgesetz (ESAEG), in force since 2015. The parameters every controller must know cold:

5.3 The DGS is not only a payout vehicle — Art. 109

It is tempting to file the DGS as "the thing that pays the depositor in a liquidation." That is half the story. Under BRRD Art. 109, where covered deposits are within the scope of bail-in and a resolution preserves access to them, the DGS can be required to contribute in the resolution — to the extent that covered depositors would otherwise have borne losses had the bank gone into ordinary insolvency. The contribution is capped twice: it can never exceed what the DGS would have paid out in a counterfactual liquidation (the NCWO bound), and it is subject to a ceiling of 50% of the DGS's target level (the Member State may raise this). So the DGS is both a payout-in-liquidation vehicle and a resolution-financing participant — a distinction the controller must keep straight when modelling the fund's expected drawdown.

5.4 The target-level arithmetic

▶ Sizing the fund and a single bank's contribution

Suppose the sector's covered deposits total EUR 50,000m. The target fund is:

$$ 0.8\% \times 50{,}000 = \textbf{EUR 400m} $$

This is the available financial means the DGS must accumulate ex ante. A single primary bank with EUR 500m of covered deposits and a risk-based contribution multiplier — say an aggregate risk weight (ARW) of 0.75 against a sector-average annual levy rate of, illustratively, 0.05% of covered deposits — would pay roughly:

$$ 0.05\% \times 500 \times 0.75 = 0.1875\ \text{EUR m} \approx \textbf{EUR 187,500 / year} $$

The risk-based weighting (DGSD Art. 13, EBA/GL/2015/10) means a riskier member pays more for the same deposit base — the scheme is actuarially fair-ish, not flat. The exact rate and ARW formula are ÖRS modelling decision within the EBA-prescribed envelope; the 0.8% target and the risk-based principle are regulatory values. Trade-off: more risk-sensitivity sharpens incentives but raises the contribution volatility a small bank must budget for.

5.5 Depositor preference and NCWO — why the DGS is cheap to the system

The DGS pays the insured depositor, then by subrogation steps into the depositor's claim in the insolvency. Because BRRD Art. 108 gives covered deposits (and the subrogated DGS) a super-senior rank in the creditor hierarchy, the DGS recovers a large share from the estate. Its net cost is therefore far below the gross payout — which is precisely why a 0.8% fund is plausibly adequate for a sector whose individual failures are small and well-collateralised. This preference is also the mechanism that protects the NCWO counterfactual in any resolution: covered depositors are never bailed in, and the Art. 109 DGS contribution is bounded by exactly that counterfactual.

6 · Nuances & traps

6.1 The three-way interplay: R-IPS vs DGS vs resolution

The sector runs three overlapping safety mechanisms, and the viva-ready skill is sequencing them:

▶ Why this ordering matters to the Aufsichtsrat

In the Raiffeisen world the R-IPS bites first and hardest: a healthy IPS means a member rarely reaches FOLTF, so the DGS rarely pays and resolution essentially never happens. The IPS is both a recovery tool (it can recapitalise pre-failure) and, where recognised, can fold the DGS function into the same legal vehicle. The defensible board narrative: "We do not rely on the State's resolution tools because we have priced and pre-funded mutual support that stops failures upstream — before even supervisory early intervention is needed." That is the proportionality bargain — and it is why MREL is near-absent at the primary level (§3.3).

⚠ Recovery plan ≠ early intervention ≠ resolution plan

The Sanierungsplan is written by the bank, assumes survival, and is approved by the supervisor. Early intervention (Art. 27–29) is a supervisory power exercised while the bank is still a going concern but deteriorating — up to removing management or installing a vorläufiger Verwalter. The Abwicklungsplan (resolution plan) is written by the resolution authority, assumes failure, and the bank merely supplies data. Conflating them — or assuming a recovery indicator breach triggers resolution — is a classic exam error. A recovery-indicator breach triggers management escalation; deterioration may trigger early intervention; only FOLTF + PIA triggers resolution. Different documents, different authors, different worlds.

⚠ "MREL is just more capital" — no

MREL eligibility is not the same as own-funds eligibility, and it has its own gates (§3.3): not funded by the institution, not subject to set-off, residual maturity > 1 year, no early redemption without authority consent, plus the retail-sale restriction on subordinated eligible liabilities. An instrument can count as MREL without being CET1/AT1/T2 (e.g. certain senior non-preferred or eligible senior liabilities), and conversely the same CET1 cannot be double-counted against both MREL and the CBR (§3.4). Treat MREL as a distinct stack with its own eligibility, maturity and subordination rules — not as a relabelled total-capital ratio.

⚠ Don't hard-code the MCC, the leverage leg, or "RWA always binds"

Three calibration shortcuts to avoid. (1) The MCC is the SRB default of CBR − 125bps (floored at zero), not CBR − CCyB; the two coincide only when CCyB happens to equal 125bps. (2) The leverage-leg MREL is calibrated to restore the leverage ratio requirement, not a mechanical 2× the LRR. (3) Always compute both legs and take the higher — in a low-risk-weight, deposit-funded sector book the leverage leg can bind, so never assume the risk-weighted leg wins.

⚠ The PIA can flip

A negative PIA is not permanent — and it is not driven by size alone. Consolidation, growth, or a change in the substitutability of a bank's functions can move it from a liquidation strategy to a resolution strategy — at which point a real MREL appears and the §4 arithmetic, including subordinated-debt issuance and the retail-sale restriction, becomes a live funding obligation. The controller should track the PIA assumption as a model input, not a constant.

▶ Merksatz

Sanierung rettet die Bank von innen; frühzeitiges Eingreifen lässt die Aufsicht im laufenden Betrieb durchgreifen; Abwicklung wickelt sie geordnet ab; die Regelinsolvenz plus Einlagensicherung fängt den Rest. Rechtsgrundlage in Österreich: BaSAG (BRRD) und ESAEG (DGSD). Für die meisten Raiffeisen-Primärbanken gilt: negative PIA → Liquidationsstrategie → faktisch kein externes MREL, dafür R-IPS und ESAEG als Sicherheitsnetz.

Recap

  • Austria transposes BRRD/BRRD2 in the BaSAG (the FMA's recovery, early-intervention and resolution powers) and the DGSD in the ESAEG — these are the operative national statutes a sector controller cites.
  • The escalation ladder has a middle rung: recovery-indicator breach → management escalation → supervisory early intervention (BRRD Art. 27–29: implement the recovery plan, replace management, appoint a temporary administrator) → FOLTF → resolution or liquidation.
  • Resolution applies only on FOLTF plus a positive public-interest assessment; the PIA, not size, decides — most Raiffeisen primary banks fail it (substitutable functions, DGS-protected deposits) and so carry a liquidation strategy with essentially no external MREL beyond own funds (post-Directive 2024/1174).
  • MREL = LAA (P1+P2R) + RCA (P1+P2R+MCC); the SRB-default MCC is CBR − 125bps (not CBR − CCyB), and the leverage leg is calibrated to restore the leverage ratio requirement (not a mechanical 2× LRR). Compute both legs and take the higher.
  • The M-MDA check is CET1-specific: apply non-CET1 eligible debt to MREL first, then CET1 fills the rest of MREL, and only the residual CET1 is tested against the CBR — never compare total instruments against a stacked MREL+CBR figure (that double-counts non-CET1 debt against the buffer).
  • MREL eligibility (CRR Art. 72b / BRRD Art. 45b): not self-funded, no set-off, residual maturity > 1 year, no early redemption without consent, plus the retail-sale restriction on subordinated eligible liabilities — relevant in Raiffeisen distribution.
  • DGSD/ESAEG: EUR 100,000 cover, 0.8%-of-covered-deposits target, ex-ante risk-based contributions, 7-working-day payout, depositor preference — and, under BRRD Art. 109, a capped DGS contribution in resolution (NCWO counterfactual / ≤ 50% of target level).
