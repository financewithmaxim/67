Module 7 — CVA risk (CRR Title VI): the regulatory charge and the 0,71 / 1,33 scalars, derived

This is a short module with one precise job: take the regulatory CVA own-funds requirement as given, and then derive — not assert — the two scaling factors 0,71 and 1,33 that turn it into the Problemfall and Extremfall risk figures. The whole derivation is a single application of VaR vertical consistency from Module 4, resting on one assumption (normality) that you must be able to name and critique.

By the end of this module you can…

  • Define CVA risk precisely and separate it from counterparty default risk and from the bank's own DVA.
  • Explain why the Leitfaden imports the regulatory CVA charge (PosNr 7516000, 8% weight) rather than modelling CVA from scratch.
  • Derive \(0{,}71=\Phi^{-1}(0{,}95)/\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)\) and \(1{,}33=\Phi^{-1}(0{,}999)/\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)\) and confirm the arithmetic.
  • State what the normal-distribution assumption trades off — and why 1,33 may understate fat tails.
  • Recognise that the same scalar machinery drives the trading-book Preisrisiko (M6) and, conceptually, op-risk (M8) — one formula, three uses.

1 · Motivation — why CVA risk exists at all

When you hold a derivative with positive market value, that value is a receivable from your counterparty. Standard practice prices the derivative off the "risk-free" (swap/OIS) curve — so the headline mark-to-market (MTM) pretends the counterparty will always pay. It will not, with some probability. The CVA (Credit Valuation Adjustment) is exactly that correction: the value adjustment of derivative receivables for the counterparty's credit risk — the difference between the risk-free value and the value that accounts for possible default.

CVA risk is the volatility of that adjustment. Specifically, it is the potential MTM loss from a widening of the counterparty's credit spread. If the counterparty's spread blows out (its CDS gets more expensive, its perceived creditworthiness deteriorates) but it has not defaulted, the CVA you must book grows, and the net value of your position falls. If you closed the position early, that wider spread is a real cost. This is a spread-driven, mark-to-market loss — not a default event.

▶ The crisis lesson behind Title VI

In 2007–2009 roughly two-thirds of counterparty-credit losses came not from outright defaults but from CVA mark-to-market swings as spreads widened. Basel III answered with a dedicated CVA capital charge, transposed into EU law as CRR Title VI (Art. 381–386). The Leitfaden's CVA risk plugs straight into that regulatory charge — so understanding the charge is understanding the module.

2 · Concept & logic — three things people confuse

The single most common error here is conflating three distinct risks. Keep them apart:

RiskWhat triggers the lossWhere it lives
CVA risk (this module)Counterparty's credit spread widens → CVA grows → MTM falls. No default needed.CRR Title VI; here §5.2 of the Leitfaden
Default / counterparty riskThe counterparty actually defaults and the receivable is lost.Credit risk (Module 5), via EAD/exposure of derivatives
DVA (Debit Valuation Adjustment)The bank's own spread widens → its own liabilities are worth less → a paper gain.Excluded from the regulatory CVA charge

CVA risk is the spread sensitivity of the receivable; default risk is the jump-to-default of the same receivable; DVA is the symmetric adjustment on the bank's own credit — deliberately left out of the prudential charge (you cannot prudently count a "gain" from your own deterioration). The Leitfaden follows the regulatory scope: it captures CVA risk through the own-funds requirement and leaves counterparty default to the credit-risk module (where derivative exposures already enter the EAD).

One-line definition to memorise

CVA risk = potential mark-to-market loss on derivative receivables caused by a widening of the counterparty's credit spread, the position not having defaulted. It is a market-risk-flavoured charge on a credit-driven price.

3 · The Leitfaden's pragmatic approach — import the regulatory charge

Modelling CVA from first principles is hard: you need expected-exposure profiles per netting set, counterparty CDS spreads or proxies, and a full sensitivity (or simulation) engine. That is data-hungry and impossible to standardise across 400 Raiffeisenbanken. So the Leitfaden makes a deliberate proportionality call: it does not re-model CVA. Instead it takes the regulatory CVA own-funds requirement directly from the supervisory report.

The basis is the COREP position "Gesamtbetrag der Risikoposition für Anpassung der Kreditbewertung" (total risk-exposure amount for credit valuation adjustment), PosNr 7516000, carrying the standard 8% own-funds weight (Eigenmittelgewicht). The CVA capital requirement is therefore simply:

$$ \text{CVA-Eigenmittelerfordernis} \;=\; 8\% \times \underbrace{\text{Risikopositionsbetrag (PosNr 7516000)}}_{\text{regulatory CVA risk-exposure amount}} $$
The 8% is the universal CRR own-funds-to-RWA factor regulatory: the supervisory report already delivers a risk-exposure amount (an RWA-equivalent), and 8% of it is the capital charge. The risk-exposure amount itself is produced by the CRR Title VI standardised CVA method regulatory. The Leitfaden's decision to reuse this number rather than build its own CVA model is the proportionality choice ÖRS choice.

4 · Derivation — where 0,71 and 1,33 come from

The CVA-Eigenmittelerfordernis is a capital number, but it is calibrated to one specific confidence. The standardised regulatory CVA charge is built to a 99% confidence level. The Leitfaden's RTFA, however, needs the figure at 95% (Problemfall) and 99,9% (Extremfall). To move between confidences it uses exactly the VaR vertical consistency rule derived in Module 4.

4.1 The vertical-consistency engine (recap from M4)

Under a normal/parametric assumption a VaR is a quantile multiple of a volatility: \(\text{VaR}_q=\Phi^{-1}(q)\cdot\sigma\). The volatility \(\sigma\) is the same regardless of which confidence you read; only the quantile multiplier changes. So re-scaling confidence is a pure ratio of standard-normal quantiles:

$$ \text{VaR}_{q_2} \;=\; \text{VaR}_{q_1}\cdot\frac{\Phi^{-1}(q_2)}{\Phi^{-1}(q_1)}. $$
\(\Phi^{-1}\) = standard-normal inverse CDF. The \(\sigma\) cancels: \(\dfrac{\Phi^{-1}(q_2)\,\sigma}{\Phi^{-1}(q_1)\,\sigma}=\dfrac{\Phi^{-1}(q_2)}{\Phi^{-1}(q_1)}\). The quantiles you reuse constantly: \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}95)=1{,}6449\); \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)=2{,}3263\); \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}999)=3{,}0902\).

4.2 Apply it: the regulatory CVA base is at 99%

Here the base confidence is \(q_1=99\%\) (the regulatory CVA calibration), and the two targets are \(q_2=95\%\) and \(q_2=99{,}9\%\). Substituting directly:

$$ \underbrace{0{,}71}_{\text{Problemfall scalar}} \;=\; \frac{\Phi^{-1}(0{,}95)}{\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)} \;=\; \frac{1{,}6449}{2{,}3263} \;=\; 0{,}7071 \;\longrightarrow\; 0{,}71. $$ $$ \underbrace{1{,}33}_{\text{Extremfall scalar}} \;=\; \frac{\Phi^{-1}(0{,}999)}{\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)} \;=\; \frac{3{,}0902}{2{,}3263} \;=\; 1{,}3284 \;\longrightarrow\; 1{,}33. $$
Both numbers are rounded to two decimals: \(0{,}7071\to0{,}71\) and \(1{,}3284\to1{,}33\). Note the Problemfall scalar is below 1 (95% < 99%, so less capital) and the Extremfall scalar is above 1 (99,9% > 99%, more capital). The 99% base is fixed by the regulatory CVA calibration; the decision to scale it by these normal-quantile ratios is the ÖRS choice.

So the two scenario risk figures are:

$$ \text{CVA Problemfall} = \text{CVA-EM-Erfordernis}\times 0{,}71, \qquad \text{CVA Extremfall} = \text{CVA-EM-Erfordernis}\times 1{,}33. $$
🟣 What is regulatory here vs what is the ÖRS simplification

regulatory The CVA risk-exposure amount (PosNr 7516000), the 8% weight, and the 99% confidence at which the standardised CVA charge is calibrated are all CRR Title VI — fixed.   ÖRS choice Two decisions belong to the Leitfaden: (1) importing the regulatory charge instead of modelling CVA, and (2) re-scaling confidence by a normal-quantile ratio (0,71 / 1,33). The normality is the simplification — see the trap below.

5 · Worked example

Worked example — from PosNr 7516000 to both scenarios

Suppose the supervisory report shows a CVA risk-exposure amount (PosNr 7516000) of €50.000.000.

Step 1 — the regulatory CVA capital requirement (8%).

$$ \text{CVA-EM-Erfordernis} = 8\%\times \text{€50.000.000} = \text{€4.000.000}. $$

Step 2 — Problemfall (×0,71).

$$ \text{CVA Problemfall} = \text{€4.000.000}\times 0{,}71 = \textbf{€2.840.000}. $$

Step 3 — Extremfall (×1,33).

$$ \text{CVA Extremfall} = \text{€4.000.000}\times 1{,}33 = \textbf{€5.320.000}. $$

That is the entire CVA computation in the RTFA: one COREP figure, one regulatory weight, and two quantile-ratio scalars. The Extremfall is not "3× the Problemfall" (that rule-of-thumb belongs to Beteiligungsrisiko) — here the ratio is fixed at \(1{,}33/0{,}71 = 3{,}0902/1{,}6449 = 1{,}88\), the 99,9%/95% quantile ratio.

6 · In practice — the data, the unifying pattern, and the CRR III flag

6.1 Where the input comes from

PosNr 7516000 is a COREP line: the Gesamtbetrag der Risikoposition für Anpassung der Kreditbewertung reported under the CRR's own-funds requirements (C 25.00 in EBA COREP). You do not compute it — it is already produced by the bank's regulatory reporting engine. Your job in Risk Controlling is to pick up that single number, apply 8%, and apply the two scalars. No CDS curves, no exposure simulation, no proxy spreads enter the Leitfaden's version.

6.2 The unifying pattern — one formula, three uses

The scalars 0,71 and 1,33 have nothing intrinsically to do with CVA. They are pure confidence-rescaling factors off a 99% base, and the same machinery appears elsewhere:

  • CVA (this module): 99% regulatory charge × 0,71 / 1,33.
  • Trading-book Preisrisiko (Module 6): the own-funds charge (position/FX/commodity risk, minus the FX charge) is scaled by the identical 0,71 / 1,33 — same 99% base, same normal-quantile ratios.
  • Operational risk (Module 8): conceptually the same idea — a regulatory 99,9% anchor (15% of Betriebserträge) re-scaled to 95% — though op-risk uses a mixed normal/lognormal assumption (giving the 5,0% factor), precisely because a pure normal scaling understates op-risk tails.
The point of the whole module

Once you see 0,71 and 1,33 as \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}95)/\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)\) and \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}999)/\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)\), they stop being "CVA constants" and become general vertical-consistency scalars. Memorise the formula, not the numbers — they are reused for the trading book verbatim.

6.3 CRR III / Basel IV flag

⚖️ The CVA framework is being revised — verify before quoting

The current CRR Title VI standardised CVA method is replaced under Basel IV / CRR III by the new BA-CVA (Basic Approach) and SA-CVA (Standardised Approach), with EU application from 1 Jan 2025. The Leitfaden (09/2024) therefore rests on the pre-CRR III "8% × risk-exposure amount" basis, which post-dates the guide and will change: the risk-exposure amount feeding PosNr 7516000 is recalibrated under the revised framework, even though the Leitfaden's scaling step (0,71 / 1,33) is unaffected because it is pure quantile arithmetic. verify against CRR III

7 · Nuances & traps

⚠ 0,71 and 1,33 are NOT about CVA

They are pure confidence-rescaling factors off a 99% base and are reused verbatim for the trading-book Preisrisiko (M6). If an exam asks "what is special about 0,71 for CVA?", the answer is nothing — it is \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}95)/\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)\). Treating them as CVA-specific constants is the classic misread.

⚠ The normal assumption understates fat tails

The whole 0,71 / 1,33 derivation assumes the CVA loss is normally distributed, so that VaR is just \(\Phi^{-1}(q)\,\sigma\). Real credit-spread distributions are fat-tailed (spreads gap and jump in stress). Under a fat-tailed distribution the true 99,9%/99% quantile ratio is larger than 1,33 — so 1,33 may understate the genuine extreme tail of CVA risk. The trade-off is deliberate: the Leitfaden buys a one-multiplication, fully auditable scaling at the cost of tail accuracy, justified by proportionality and the fact that CVA is rarely the binding risk for a Raiffeisenbank. Note op-risk (M8) does not make this simplification — it blends a lognormal precisely to respect tails.

⚠ Don't confuse CVA risk with default risk or DVA

CVA risk = spread-driven MTM loss with no default. Counterparty default (jump-to-default of the receivable) is handled in credit risk (M5) via the derivative's EAD/exposure — booking it here too would double-count. And the bank's own-spread adjustment (DVA) is excluded from the regulatory charge entirely; you may not net a "gain" from your own deterioration into the CVA risk figure.

Recap

  • CVA risk = potential MTM loss on derivative receivables from a widening of the counterparty's credit spread (no default) — distinct from counterparty default risk (M5) and from the bank's own DVA (excluded). Basis: CRR Title VI (Art. 381–386).
  • The Leitfaden imports the regulatory charge: CVA-EM-Erfordernis = 8% × PosNr 7516000 rather than modelling CVA itself.
  • The regulatory CVA charge is calibrated at 99%. Vertical consistency rescales it: Problemfall ×0,71 = \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}95)/\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)=1{,}6449/2{,}3263=0{,}7071\); Extremfall ×1,33 = \(\Phi^{-1}(0{,}999)/\Phi^{-1}(0{,}99)=3{,}0902/2{,}3263=1{,}3284\).
  • The scalars are not CVA-specific — the trading-book Preisrisiko (M6) uses the identical 0,71 / 1,33, and op-risk (M8) the same idea on a 99,9% anchor. One formula, three uses.
  • The normal assumption understates fat tails ⇒ 1,33 may understate the true extreme; and the underlying CVA framework is revised under CRR III (BA-CVA / SA-CVA) — verify.
